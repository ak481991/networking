01AAA & identity

Identity is where most enterprise security projects live, so these come up constantly. The discriminating answers are the EAP method comparison, why MAB is a compensating control rather than authentication, and what CoA is for.

Compare RADIUS and TACACS+. When do you use each?Core

RADIUS is UDP, encrypts only the password field, and combines authentication with authorisation — it is the protocol for network access control. TACACS+ is TCP, encrypts the entire payload, and separates authentication, authorisation and accounting, which is what makes it the right choice for device administration and command-level control.

The practical consequence of the separation: with TACACS+ you can authenticate an engineer centrally and still make per-command authorisation decisions, so a junior operator can run show commands and nothing else. RADIUS has no comparable mechanism — the closest is a privilege level pushed as an attribute, which is coarse.

Walk through an 802.1X authentication. Name the three roles and the message flow.Core

Three roles: the supplicant on the endpoint, the authenticator — the switch or WLC — and the authentication server, normally RADIUS. EAP runs between supplicant and server; the switch relays it, carrying EAP over EAPOL on the wire and over RADIUS to the server, without ever seeing the credentials.

Detail
  1. Port comes up in an unauthorised state. Only EAPOL is permitted.
  2. Switch sends EAPOL-Start or the supplicant sends one; switch sends EAP-Request/Identity.
  3. Supplicant replies EAP-Response/Identity with an outer identity.
  4. Switch wraps it in RADIUS Access-Request to the server.
  5. Server and supplicant run the chosen EAP method through the switch — several round trips, and for TLS-based methods this is where the tunnel is built and certificates are exchanged.
  6. Server returns Access-Accept with authorisation attributes — VLAN, dACL, SGT — or Access-Reject.
  7. Switch applies the authorisation and sends EAP-Success. The port is now authorised for that session.

The key architectural point: the switch is a relay. It does not validate credentials, does not need to understand the EAP method, and does not hold any secret belonging to the user. That is why one switch configuration supports every EAP method — the method is negotiated between the endpoint and the server.

Compare the EAP methods. Which would you deploy and why?Deep

EAP-TLS is the strongest: mutual certificate authentication, no password on the wire at all, and nothing an attacker can capture and crack offline. PEAP and EAP-TTLS tunnel a password-based inner method inside a server-authenticated TLS tunnel, which is weaker but far easier to deploy. TEAP is the modern answer where you need both machine and user authentication in one session.

PEAP is only as strong as the client's certificate validationThe whole security of PEAP rests on the supplicant verifying the server's certificate before sending credentials into the tunnel. If the client is configured to accept any certificate — which is the default on some platforms and a very common "fix" when enrolment is awkward — then anything presenting a certificate can terminate the tunnel and receive the credentials. The mitigation is to push supplicant configuration centrally with the trusted CA pinned and server-name validation on, rather than leaving it to users. If an interviewer asks why EAP-TLS is better, this is the substantive reason: there is no credential to hand over by mistake.
What to recommend
  • EAP-TLS for managed corporate devices, with certificates issued through MDM or auto-enrolment. The deployment cost is the PKI and the lifecycle, and it is worth paying.
  • TEAP where you need machine and user in one session — so policy can distinguish "a corporate laptop with a logged-in corporate user" from either alone, which is a genuinely useful authorisation condition.
  • PEAP as a transitional or BYOD method, with supplicant configuration pushed and certificate validation enforced.
  • Never EAP-MD5, and be prepared to say why: no tunnel, no server authentication, and the hash can be captured and attacked offline.
What they're testingWhether you can rank the methods by what they actually protect and name the deployment cost of the strongest one. The PEAP server-validation dependency is the specific insight; candidates who present PEAP as simply "less secure" without saying why have not configured a supplicant.
What is MAB, and why is it not really authentication?Deep

MAC Authentication Bypass uses the device's MAC address as its identity when it cannot do 802.1X. A MAC address is transmitted in the clear in every frame and is trivially changed on the endpoint, so it is an identifier rather than a credential — MAB is a compensating control, not authentication.

Detail

How it works: after 802.1X times out or fails, the switch takes the source MAC of the first frame and sends it to RADIUS as both username and password. The server checks it against an endpoint database and returns an authorisation result.

Why it is weak: the identity is public and forgeable. Anyone who can observe traffic learns valid MAC addresses, and setting a MAC is a single command on most operating systems. There is no secret involved at any point.

What makes it defensible anyway — this is the useful half of the answer:

  • Profiling. Rather than trusting the MAC alone, ISE builds a profile from DHCP fingerprints, HTTP user-agent, SNMP data, CDP/LLDP, and active probes. A device claiming to be a particular camera model should also behave like it; a mismatch is a signal.
  • Tight authorisation. A MAB-authenticated device gets the minimum it needs — a dACL or an SGT permitting only its controller and nothing else. Then a forged identity gains very little.
  • Monitoring for profile change. An endpoint whose profile changes, or whose MAC appears in a new location, is worth alerting on.
  • Prefer 802.1X wherever the device supports it, and treat the MAB population as a list to shrink rather than a steady state.

Explain the ISE policy model. What is the difference between authentication and authorisation policy?Deep

Authentication policy decides which identity store validates the credential and whether the credential is valid. Authorisation policy decides what the validated identity gets — VLAN, dACL, SGT, redirect. They are separate because the questions are separate: who you are, and what you may do.

Points worth stating:

  • Policy sets come first, and getting them right simplifies everything downstream. Separate sets for wired, wireless, VPN and device administration keep each rule set short and comprehensible.
  • Authentication failure is not the same as authorisation failure, and the distinction matters when troubleshooting. A valid certificate with no matching authorisation rule falls through to the default — so a user "fails to connect" with a perfectly successful authentication.
  • The default authorisation rule is a security decision. If it permits access, every gap in your rules becomes an open door. If it denies, every gap becomes a support ticket. Most deployments want deny, reached deliberately after a monitor-mode period.
  • Authorisation conditions are the interesting part: AD group plus endpoint profile plus posture plus location lets you express "a compliant corporate laptop with a corporate user, on a corporate site" as one rule.
Read the Live Log, not the endpointAlmost every ISE troubleshooting question is answered by the detailed authentication report for that session: which policy set matched, which authentication rule, which identity store, which authorisation rule, and the failure reason. Guessing from the endpoint's behaviour wastes time, because the symptom — "no network" — is identical for a dozen different causes. The first move on any identity problem is to find that session in the log and read which rule matched, and that is what an interviewer wants to hear.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What is a dACL versus a VLAN assignment?" — a downloadable ACL applies a filter to the session on the switch port, so the device stays in its VLAN with restricted access. VLAN assignment moves it into a different subnet, which means a DHCP renewal and breaks anything stateful. dACLs or SGTs are generally preferable; VLAN assignment is coarse and disruptive.
  • "How do you handle a RADIUS outage?" — critical authentication VLAN or inaccessible-authentication-bypass, with a deliberately chosen fallback level of access. Decide what that grants in advance, with the security team, rather than during the outage.
What they're testingWhether the two-stage model is clear to you, and whether you go to the log first. The default-authorisation-rule point is the design judgement, and the dACL-versus-VLAN preference is the deployment experience.
What is Change of Authorization, and what does it make possible?Expert

CoA lets the RADIUS server push a change to an existing session — re-authenticate it, apply a different authorisation, or terminate it — without the endpoint doing anything. Without CoA, authorisation is decided once at connection time and cannot change until the device reconnects, which makes posture assessment, profiling and quarantine impossible.

Detail

The flows CoA enables, and each is a genuine capability rather than a convenience:

  • Posture assessment. Authorise with limited access and a redirect, let the agent check compliance, then CoA to full access once the device passes. Without CoA you would have to grant access first and hope.
  • Profiling-driven authorisation. A device authenticates by MAB before its profile is known. Once DHCP and HTTP data identify it as a particular camera model, CoA applies the camera policy. The classification arrives after the session starts, so the authorisation must be able to change.
  • Quarantine on a security event. A SIEM or an EDR platform determines a host is compromised and tells ISE, which issues a CoA to move that session into a restricted authorisation or terminate it — in seconds, without touching the switch. This is the capability people actually mean by "network response".
  • Policy change applied to live sessions, rather than only to new ones.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "Why would you use port bounce rather than reauth?" — because a VLAN change requires the endpoint to get a new address, and most supplicants will not renew DHCP just because authorisation changed. Bouncing the port forces it. It is disruptive, which is one more argument for dACLs and SGTs over VLAN assignment.
  • "How fast is quarantine in practice?" — the CoA itself is immediate; the end-to-end time is dominated by detection and by whatever integration passes the signal to ISE. Being clear that the network is the fast part and detection is the slow part is a useful piece of honesty in any automated-response discussion.
What they're testingWhether you understand that authorisation has to be mutable for anything interesting to be possible. The inbound-port firewall detail is the specific deployment knowledge, and it is a very common real-world failure.

02Firewall architecture

The highest-value answers here are that an existing connection bypasses the ACL, that ASP drop reasons name the cause directly, and why a stateful firewall pair cannot tolerate asymmetric routing.

What does "stateful" actually mean? What state is kept?Core

The firewall records each permitted connection in a table and uses that record to permit the return traffic implicitly. So you write policy for one direction only, and the firewall knows that a reply belongs to a conversation it already approved rather than matching it against a rule.

An ACL change does not affect existing connectionsBecause a packet matching an existing connection takes the fast path and is never re-evaluated against the ACL, tightening a rule to deny a flow leaves that flow working for as long as it stays active. Walk through the ASA order of operations for a new inbound packet.Deep

Interface ingress, then the existing-connection lookup — which short-circuits almost everything if it hits. For a new connection: un-NAT the destination, check the ACL against real addresses, do a route lookup to pick the egress interface, apply NAT, run inspection, then egress.

The sequence
  1. Ingress interface. Layer 2 checks, and the packet enters the security path.
  2. Existing connection lookup. A hit means the fast path — no ACL re-evaluation, no new NAT decision. This is where most packets are handled.
  3. NAT untranslate. For inbound traffic to a translated address, the real destination is determined first.
  4. ACL check. On modern ASA this uses real, untranslated addresses — which changed at 8.3 and is a standing source of confusion for anyone who learned the old behaviour.
  5. Route lookup. Determines the egress interface.
  6. NAT translate. The outbound translation is applied.
  7. Inspection and service policy. MPF applies protocol inspection, policing, and on FTD hands off to Snort.
  8. Egress ACL, if one is configured on the outgoing interface.
  9. Layer 2 resolution and transmit.
The 8.3 ACL change is the detail they are checkingBefore 8.3, an inbound ACL referenced the mapped (translated) address, so you permitted traffic to the public IP. From 8.3 onward the ACL references the real address, so you permit traffic to the server's actual private IP even though the packet arrived addressed to the public one. Configurations migrated across that boundary and engineers who learned the old model both get this wrong, and the symptom is a rule that reads correctly and matches nothing. packet-tracer settles it in one command.

The prefilter policy is the performance toolSnort inspection costs CPU, and some traffic does not benefit from it — a high-volume backup stream, encrypted replication between known hosts, voice media. Compare active/standby failover, active/active, and clustering.Deep

Active/standby is one unit forwarding with a synchronised partner — simple and the default choice. Active/active splits contexts between two units so both forward, requiring multiple context mode. Clustering pools several units into one logical firewall with distributed flow ownership, which is how you scale throughput beyond a single appliance.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "What triggers a failover?" — loss of heartbeats on the failover link, a monitored interface failing, a hardware or process failure, or a manual switchover. Why does asymmetric routing break a stateful firewall, and what are the options?Expert

Because the firewall permits return traffic based on a connection record it created when it saw the outbound packet. If the forward path goes through one firewall and the return through another, the second has no record and drops the reply as a mid-stream packet — tcp-not-syn. The firewall is working exactly as designed; the topology is wrong for it.

Detail

The symptom is distinctive and frequently misdiagnosed: connections that fail only sometimes, or only for certain source and destination pairs, with the firewall logging drops for traffic that the ACL clearly permits. Because path selection is often hash-based, the same two hosts behave consistently while another pair fails consistently, so it looks like an application problem.

A site-to-site VPN comes up but no traffic passes. What is the usual NAT cause?Deep

What is hairpinning, and why does an internal user fail to reach an internal server by its public address?Deep

The reply has source 10.1.1.10, but the client is waiting for a reply from 198.51.100.10. Mismatch, dropped, connection fails.

Explain zone-based firewall on IOS. How does it differ from interface ACLs?Deep

ZBFW groups interfaces into zones and applies stateful policy to zone pairs, which are directional. Traffic between zones is denied unless a zone pair with a policy permits it, and traffic within a zone is permitted by default. Interface ACLs are stateless and per interface; ZBFW is stateful and per zone relationship, which scales far better as interfaces are added.

Detail
  • Zones contain interfaces. An interface belongs to at most one zone, and an interface in no zone cannot pass traffic to one that is in a zone.
  • Zone pairs are directional. inside to outside is a different pair from outside to inside, so you write policy for the direction you mean.
  • Default deny between zones, default permit within a zone. Adding an interface to an existing zone therefore inherits policy automatically — which is the scaling advantage.
  • The self zone is the router itself. Traffic to and from the router's own addresses is governed by zone pairs involving self, and it is permitted by default unless you create such a pair — at which point you must permit the management and routing protocols you need.

What they're testingWhether you know the default behaviours — deny between zones, permit within, and the self zone's trap. Those defaults are what make ZBFW either elegant or an outage, and candidates who have deployed it lead with them.
You inherit a firewall with 2,000 rules and no documentation. How do you clean it up?Deep

Use the hit counters. Rules with zero hits over a long enough observation window are candidates for removal, shadowed rules can be identified structurally, and anything permitting any any is a finding regardless of its hit count. The work is methodical and the main risk is removing something that is used rarely rather than never.

The method
  1. Baseline the hit counts and let them run. Clear them, then observe for long enough to cover every business cycle — a month minimum, a quarter if there is quarter-end processing, and longer if there is an annual process. This is the step people shorten and regret.
  2. Find the structural problems first, because they need no observation: rules shadowed by an earlier broader rule, duplicate rules, overlapping ranges, and any permit ip any any.
  3. Rank by risk, not by age. A broad permit rule with high hit counts is more interesting than an unused narrow one — it may be the rule everything is actually using, hiding the need for hundreds of specific ones.
  4. Disable before deleting. Where the platform supports it, disable a rule and leave it in place for a cycle. Reverting a disable is faster than reconstructing a deleted rule under pressure.
  5. Attribute what remains. Every surviving rule should have an owner, a reason and a review date recorded in its description field or in an external register. A rule nobody owns will be in the same state in three years.
  6. Change in small batches with a defined rollback, and never on a Friday.

The cost of group-based policy is the classification and propagation infrastructure, and that it is only as good as the classification.

What they're testingWhether you understand that address-based policy couples security to topology. That insight is the reason group-based policy exists, and naming the three costs keeps the answer credible rather than promotional.

What is perfect forward secrecy, and what do the SA lifetimes actually control?Deep

PFS means each child SA derives its keys from a fresh Diffie-Hellman exchange rather than from the IKE SA's existing key material. So compromise of one key does not allow decryption of past or future sessions — which also means that recorded traffic cannot be decrypted later if the long-term key is eventually compromised.

Detail

Without PFS, child SA keys are derived from the IKE SA's keying material. An attacker who recovers that material — by compromising the device, or by eventually breaking the authentication key — can derive the keys for every child SA and decrypt everything they recorded. With PFS, each child SA has independent key material from its own ephemeral DH exchange, so there is nothing to derive.

Mismatched lifetimes cause intermittent, scheduled disruptionIf the two ends disagree on lifetimes, rekeys happen at different times and one side may tear down an SA the other still considers valid. Follow-ups to expect
  • "Does PFS cost anything?" — a Diffie-Hellman exchange per rekey, which is CPU on both ends. On a device with many tunnels rekeying frequently that is measurable, which argues for sensible lifetimes rather than for disabling PFS.
  • "Which DH group?" — group 19, 20 or 21 (elliptic curve) for new deployments; group 14 as a minimum where EC is unavailable. Groups 1, 2 and 5 are too small and should be removed from any proposal list.
What they're testingWhether you can explain PFS in terms of what an attacker gains without it, and whether you know about the volume lifetime. The scheduled-disruption diagnosis is the practical payoff.
Explain NAT-T. Why is AH incompatible with NAT, and what MTU problems does IPsec cause?Deep

ESP has no port numbers, so a NAT device cannot multiplex several ESP flows behind one address. NAT-T detects a NAT in the path and encapsulates ESP in UDP 4500, giving the NAT something to translate. AH cannot be made to work at all, because its integrity check covers the outer IP header — which NAT by definition modifies.

Detail

How detection works: during IKE_SA_INIT each peer sends NAT_DETECTION payloads containing hashes of the IP addresses and ports it believes are in use. If the received hash does not match what the peer computes locally, a NAT has rewritten the header, and both sides switch to UDP 4500. Keepalives then maintain the NAT mapping so the translation does not time out.

The MTU problem: ESP in tunnel mode adds a new IP header, the ESP header, an IV, padding and an authentication trailer — of the order of 50 to 60 bytes with AES. NAT-T adds 8 more for the UDP header. GRE over IPsec adds another 24. A 1500-byte packet therefore does not fit a 1500-byte path, and the result is fragmentation or black-holed large packets.

ip tcp adjust-mss is the line that makes IPsec usableWithout it, TCP endpoints negotiate a segment size based on their local MTU, send full-size segments, and those do not fit the tunnel. Clamping the MSS makes the endpoints negotiate a size that fits, and it is the single most effective line in a tunnel configuration. A good value is the tunnel MTU minus 40.
Follow-ups to expect

  • "What is DPD for?" — detecting that the peer has gone away, so SAs are torn down and the tunnel can be re-established rather than sitting up and black-holing. IKEv2 has it built in as an informational exchange; IKEv1 needed an extension.

06VPN topologies

Scale is the theme. Every topology here exists because a full mesh of point-to-point tunnels does not scale, and each solves it differently — on-demand tunnels, a group key, or a controller distributing keys.

Explain DMVPN and its three phases. What does NHRP do?Deep

DMVPN combines multipoint GRE, NHRP and IPsec so that spokes register with a hub and can then build direct tunnels to each other on demand — without the hub having to be configured with every spoke. NHRP is the resolution protocol: it maps a tunnel address to the real public address behind it, which is what lets a spoke find a peer.

What is GETVPN and when would you use it?Deep

GETVPN encrypts traffic without building tunnels. A key server distributes a shared group key to all members, and each member encrypts using IPsec while preserving the original IP header — so routing, multicast and any-to-any reachability work exactly as they did before encryption. It is for encrypting a private WAN, typically MPLS, not the internet.

Why header preservation is the whole point: because the original source and destination addresses survive encryption, the provider's network still routes the packet normally, multicast still replicates, and QoS classification on the outer header still works. And why it cannot be used on the internet: header preservation means the real private addresses appear in the outer header, which is neither routable nor NAT-compatible across the public internet. GETVPN requires a network that already routes your addresses — an MPLS VPN or private WAN.

Design a remote-access VPN. Split tunnel or tunnel everything?Deep

It is a trade between inspection and capacity. Tunnelling everything means all traffic is inspected and logged, at the cost of hauling internet traffic through the corporate edge. Split tunnelling sends only corporate traffic down the tunnel, which is far more efficient and means the endpoint's internet traffic is uninspected — so the endpoint's own controls have to be good enough.

The rest of the design
  • Authentication: certificate for the device plus user credentials with MFA. Certificate alone identifies the machine; MFA protects against credential theft, which is the dominant attack on remote access.
  • Posture assessment before granting full access — patch level, disk encryption, EDR agent running — applied with a limited initial authorisation and a CoA once compliance is confirmed.
  • Authorisation by group, so contractors, employees and administrators get different access from the same headend.
  • DTLS for the data channel where available, because TCP-over-TCP performs badly; TLS as the fallback when UDP is blocked.
  • Capacity and redundancy: two headends in different locations, sized for the peak concurrent count with one headend lost — which is the sizing rule everyone discovered in 2020.
  • Always-on with captive-portal detection for managed devices, so the tunnel establishes before the user does anything.
  • Split DNS, so internal names resolve internally without tunnelling all DNS.

How does SD-WAN handle encryption and trust differently from traditional IPsec?Deep

There is no IKE between edge devices. Devices authenticate to the controllers with certificates over DTLS, and the controller distributes pairwise data-plane keys through the overlay control protocol. That removes the n-squared IKE problem entirely — adding a site means one control connection, not a tunnel negotiation with every other site.

Why removing IKE matters: in a traditional full-mesh IPsec design, every pair of sites negotiates and maintains its own IKE session, so a hundred sites is nearly five thousand sessions to establish, rekey and monitor. With controller-distributed keys, each edge maintains control connections to the controllers and receives the key material it needs. Scaling becomes linear, and rekeying is a controller operation rather than a mesh-wide renegotiation.

The onboarding model is also distinctive: a device is only admitted if it presents a valid certificate and its identity appears in an authorised device list. So possessing a valid certificate is not sufficient — a stolen or cloned device still has to be on the list, and removing it from the list revokes it immediately without waiting for certificate revocation to propagate.

Two failures account for most certificate incidentsThe clock. A device whose time is wrong — a router that booted without NTP, an appliance with a dead battery — will reject every valid certificate as not-yet-valid or expired, and the error message points at the certificate rather than at the clock. Follow-ups to expect
  • "Why is CN no longer used for name matching?" — it was never specified for that purpose and it could hold only one name. Compare CRL and OCSP. What is the soft-fail problem?Deep

A CRL is a signed list of revoked serial numbers that clients download periodically — simple, cacheable, and stale between updates. OCSP queries the status of one certificate on demand, which is fresher but depends on a responder being reachable. The soft-fail problem is that when the check cannot be completed, most implementations accept the certificate anyway — so revocation can be defeated simply by making the responder unreachable.

The soft-fail problem in detail: if the CRL distribution point or the OCSP responder is unreachable, the validator has a choice. Hard-fail means reject the certificate — secure, and it means a responder outage becomes an authentication outage for everything. Soft-fail means accept — available, and it means revocation provides no protection against an attacker who can block the check, which an attacker positioned to use a stolen certificate generally can. Almost every default is soft-fail, for availability reasons.

It is the right answer for TLS services.
  • "How do you revoke in a hurry?" — revoking the certificate is only half of it, because propagation depends on CRL intervals and caching. Knowing that authorisation beats revocation for speed is a genuinely useful operational insight.

Explain TrustSec. What are the three stages?Deep

Every TrustSec problem is a failure in one of those three, and identifying which one is the first troubleshooting step.

What is the difference between macro and micro segmentation? When do you use a VRF rather than a group tag?Deep

Macro segmentation separates routing domains, so there is no path between them at all unless you deliberately build one — VRFs, or virtual networks in a fabric. Micro segmentation controls traffic within a routing domain by group, so hosts that can reach each other at layer 3 are permitted or denied by policy. You use a VRF when the separation should be absolute, and a tag when it should be policy.

The failure-mode argument decides the hard casesMacro segmentation fails closed: if classification is wrong, the host has no path and somebody raises a ticket. So for anything where a classification failure must not grant access — a cardholder data environment, an OT network, a regulated zone — put a VRF boundary and a firewall around it, and use group policy for the finer control inside. Choosing the mechanism by its failure mode rather than by its granularity is the senior answer.

What does zero trust actually mean for a network team?Expert

That network location stops conferring trust. Being on the corporate LAN grants nothing by itself; every session is authenticated and authorised on its own merits, continuously, with least privilege. For a network team that translates into four concrete things: identity at every port, segmentation by identity rather than topology, encryption of traffic that used to be trusted because it was internal, and telemetry sufficient to verify all of it.

You have been asked to segment a flat network. How do you do it without causing outages?Expert

Discovery first, enforcement last. You cannot write policy for traffic you have not observed, so the first phase is classification plus flow collection with no enforcement at all. Then policy in monitor mode, so you can see what would have been denied. Only then enforce, starting at the boundaries where the dependency map is simplest.

The phases
  1. Classify without enforcing. Deploy 802.1X in monitor mode and assign tags, but enforce nothing. You now know what everything is, and nothing has changed for users.
  2. Collect flows. NetFlow or IPFIX everywhere relevant, for long enough to cover the business cycle. The deliverable is a dependency map: which groups talk to which, on what ports, how often. This is the phase that determines whether the project succeeds.
  3. Write policy from the data, not from assumptions. The observed flows tell you what the permit rules must be. Everything else is a candidate for denial.
  4. Enforce in monitor mode. SGACL monitor mode logs what would have been dropped without dropping it. Run it for a full cycle and investigate every hit — each one is either a missing rule or something that genuinely should be blocked.
  5. Enforce at the easiest boundary first. Guest, IoT, contractor and printers have small, well-understood dependency sets. Start there, get the operational process working, build confidence.
  6. Work inward. Corporate-to-corporate and server-to-server last, because those dependency maps are the largest and least documented.
  7. Keep a rollback at every step, and change in increments small enough that a problem is obviously attributable.

A CoPP policy built from recommended numbers rather than measured ones is how you discover that the default class was carrying something essential, and the symptom is a device that loses adjacencies or management access under load with no obvious cause. CoPP drops are also invisible to any packet capture on the wire, which makes the resulting fault very hard to diagnose.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What is CPPr?" — Control Plane Protection, which subdivides the control plane into host, transit and CEF-exception paths so you can police them separately, plus port filtering to drop traffic to closed ports before it costs anything. More granular than CoPP and worth knowing exists.
  • "How would you know CoPP was causing a problem?" — the class drop counters. A protocol failing while the wire capture is clean, with a non-zero CoPP drop count on the relevant class, is conclusive. That is the same reasoning as CoPP on a Nexus or punt policer drops on a Catalyst, and the lesson generalises: a clean capture plus a policer drop means the packet arrived and was discarded on the way to the CPU.
What they're testingWhether you can design a real policy and whether you know to measure before dropping. The transmit-first rollout is the operational discipline, and it is what separates someone who has deployed CoPP from someone who has copied a template.

How would you detect lateral movement using network telemetry?Expert

Look for changes in which hosts talk to which. Lateral movement means a host starting conversations it has never had before — new peers, administrative protocols to many destinations, authentication attempts spread across hosts. The signal is almost always a change in the peer set rather than any individual connection being suspicious, which is why a baseline is the prerequisite.

What makes it actionable: flow data alone gives you an IP address and a pattern. Correlating with the identity layer — which authenticated session held that address at that time — and with endpoint telemetry turns it into "this user on this device did this", which is what an investigation needs. The network provides the breadth; identity and endpoint provide the attribution.

Segmentation turns policy into detectionThis is the point worth making. In a flat network, lateral movement is indistinguishable from normal traffic because everything can talk to everything. Once segmentation is in place, an attempt to cross a boundary produces a policy denial — and a denial is a far higher-fidelity signal than a statistical anomaly, because it is a specific thing that should not have happened. So segmentation is a detection investment as well as a prevention one, and SGACL or firewall denies between internal zones belong in your alerting. That reframing — segmentation generates signal, not just control — is a strong answer.
Follow-ups to expect

  • "Where would you place sensors?" — at segment boundaries and in the data centre east-west path, not just at the internet edge. Perimeter-only telemetry sees the initial compromise and the exfiltration and misses everything in between, which is where the time to detect is lost.
What they're testingWhether you think in baselines and changes rather than signatures, and whether you connect segmentation to detection. The false-positive honesty about scanners is what makes the answer practical.

How do you approach a security escalation differently from a network fault?Core

Three differences. There may be an adversary reacting to what you do, so your actions have consequences beyond the technical. The evidence is perishable and may be needed later, so collection comes before remediation. And the communication path is different — more people need to know, some things must not be written down speculatively, and there are decisions that are not yours.

What to do
  • Scope before acting. One host or many; one site or the estate. Containing part of a problem can be worse than containing none of it.
  • Preserve first, remediate second, where it costs minutes rather than hours.
  • Keep a contemporaneous timeline with timestamps and who did what. It becomes the incident record and nobody can reconstruct it later.
  • Record facts, not theories. "At 14:02 flow data shows host X connecting to Y" rather than "X was compromised by Y" — the second is an inference and may be wrong, and incident notes get read by people who will treat it as established.
  • Escalate the decisions that are not yours: whether to contain or observe, whether to notify, whether to involve law enforcement.
  • One source of truth for status, so that six people are not maintaining six versions of what is happening.

That single discipline is both professionally correct and rare, and it signals that you have been through an incident with consequences rather than only a technical one. The companion point: do not go quiet. Regular short status updates, even "no change, still investigating", prevent the escalation spiral where management starts asking other people.
What they're testingWhether you understand that a security incident has non-technical dimensions you must respect. The decision-rights point and the facts-versus-inference discipline are what interviewers for escalation roles are listening for.
"Tell me about a security incident you were involved in." How should you answer?Core

Pick one where your contribution was identifiable and the technical work was interesting, and structure it as: what was detected and how, what made it hard, what you did, what the outcome was, and what changed afterwards. Be careful about detail — describe your actions and reasoning without disclosing your employer's or customer's specifics.

Structure
  1. How it surfaced. An alert, a user report, a third-party notification, something you noticed. One sentence.
  2. What made it hard. Misleading evidence, limited visibility, pressure to restore, a gap in telemetry. This is where the story gets interesting.
  3. What you contributed specifically. Be concrete and own your part. "I pulled the flow records and established that three other hosts had contacted the same destination, which changed the scope assessment."
  4. How it was contained and resolved, and who decided what.
  5. What changed afterwards. Telemetry added, segmentation implemented, a detection written, a process fixed. This is the part that matters most.
Handle confidentiality explicitly and earlySay something like "I will describe the technical shape and my actions without identifying the organisation or the specifics." That is the professionally correct answer and interviewers notice it positively — a candidate who volunteers a customer's incident in identifiable detail has told them something about how they will handle their future employer's. You can be entirely concrete about your reasoning and actions while being vague about whose network it was, and that is the balance to aim for.
What makes a weak answer
  • No personal contribution. "The security team handled it" tells the interviewer nothing about you.
  • No prevention step. If nothing changed afterwards, either the story is incomplete or the organisation learned nothing, and neither reflects well.
  • Over-claiming. Describing yourself as having single-handedly resolved something that was obviously a team effort reads badly and invites probing questions you cannot answer.
  • Blaming people. "The server team had left it unpatched" may be true and it is the wrong register. Describe the condition, not the culprit.
  • Too much identifiable detail. As above.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What would you do differently?" — have a real, specific answer. "We spent the first hour on the firewall logs when the flow data would have scoped it in ten minutes, because nobody had built the flow queries in advance." Concrete, honest, and it shows reflection.
  • "What did you learn about the organisation's readiness?" — a good opportunity to talk about gaps you then closed: missing east-west telemetry, no pre-authorised containment process, log retention too short, no tested out-of-band access. Framing a past weakness as something you fixed is the strongest version of this answer.
What they're testingJudgement, communication, and discretion. The confidentiality handling is assessed whether or not it is mentioned, and the "what changed afterwards" answer is what separates someone who responds to incidents from someone who reduces them.

Design a secure internet edge. Talk me through the layers.Deep

Ask first what is being published, what the outbound requirements are, whether remote access terminates here, and whether there is a regulatory requirement for full inspection. Then build it as layers with different jobs: router protecting itself and the circuit, firewall doing stateful policy and NAT, inspection doing layer 7, and DMZs separating anything published from everything internal.

The layers
  1. Border router. Infrastructure ACL denying traffic to your own infrastructure addresses, strict uRPF, CoPP, no unnecessary services, BGP hardened with authentication, TTL security, maximum-prefix and the ^$ outbound filter. Its job is to protect itself and the circuit, not to be the firewall.
  2. Firewall pair. Stateful policy, NAT, and the published services. Active/standby with a stateful failover link unless throughput demands clustering. Default deny inbound; outbound policy that is actually restrictive rather than permit ip any any.
  3. DMZ interfaces, plural. Separate zones for different exposure levels — published web, published mail, partner-facing — so compromise of one does not reach the others. A single flat DMZ is a common weakness.
  4. Layer 7 inspection on the flows that warrant it: IPS for inbound published services, URL and malware inspection for outbound user traffic. Prefilter the high-volume flows that gain nothing from it.
  5. Remote access terminated on its own interface or its own device, with certificates plus MFA and posture, and its own authorisation per user group.
  6. Telemetry. Unsampled flow export at the edge, firewall logs to a collector, DNS logs, and authenticated NTP so it all correlates.
  7. Out-of-band management for everything in the edge, because the incident that takes the edge down also takes your in-band access.

The point most candidates miss: outbound policyNearly every edge design puts careful thought into what comes in and allows essentially anything out. But outbound is how command-and-control and exfiltration work, so a restrictive outbound policy — forced proxy for web, DNS only to your resolvers, no direct outbound from servers, egress filtering so spoofed sources cannot leave — is one of the highest-value controls available and it costs nothing in hardware. Raising it unprompted is a strong move, because it demonstrates that your threat model includes what happens after an initial compromise rather than stopping at the perimeter.
What they're testingWhether you layer with intent, whether you separate DMZ zones, and whether you think about outbound. The out-of-band management point and the "the router is not the firewall" distinction both signal operational experience.

The discovery that happens in monitor mode is the entire value, because the endpoint inventory is always wrong.

200 branches, internet and MPLS at each. Design the connectivity and security.Deep

Ask what the branches need to reach, whether internet traffic should break out locally or be backhauled, what the application mix is, and who operates the branches. The answer is usually an SD-WAN overlay with local internet breakout through a cloud security service, because backhauling all internet traffic to a data centre is the thing that does not scale across 200 sites.

The design
  • SD-WAN overlay across both transports. IPsec between sites over internet and MPLS, with application-aware path selection so critical traffic prefers MPLS and bulk uses the internet. Transport independence is the point — you can change a circuit without changing the design.
  • Local internet breakout for general web traffic, through a cloud security service that provides URL filtering, malware inspection and DNS-layer controls. That removes the backhaul bandwidth and the latency.
  • Backhaul what genuinely needs central inspection — and name it explicitly rather than defaulting to everything.
  • Embedded security at the branch edge for east-west and server-to-branch policy: a stateful firewall and basic IPS on the edge device, rather than a separate appliance per branch.
  • Segmentation carried in the overlay. Separate VPNs or VRFs for corporate, guest, payment and building systems, so a compromised till or thermostat cannot reach corporate — and that separation is enforced in the overlay rather than depending on 200 sets of local ACLs.
  • Identity at the branch. 802.1X with RADIUS to the central deployment, with a defined fallback for when the WAN is down — which is the scenario that matters for a branch.
  • Zero-touch onboarding, because 200 sites cannot be configured individually. Device certificates plus an authorised-serial list, with policy pushed from the controller.
  • Telemetry centralised: flow data, logs and path quality from every site into one place, because you will never log into a branch router to investigate a trend.

  • "Where does the payment or regulated traffic go?" — its own segment end to end, with its own policy, and usually not through local breakout. Scoping the regulated environment tightly is what keeps the compliance burden on a small part of the estate rather than all of it.

Users report intermittent 802.1X authentication failures. Nothing changed. Diagnose it.Expert

Find the pattern before forming a theory. The live log grouped by failure reason, by switch, by site, by endpoint type and by time of day almost always reveals a cluster — one switch, one OS version, one location, or a time correlation. "Intermittent and everywhere" is usually "specific and nobody has grouped the data yet".

The causes worth knowing
  • MTU and fragmentation on the RADIUS path. EAP-TLS with a long certificate chain produces large RADIUS packets that fragment, and fragments get dropped by a firewall or a path with a smaller MTU. The failure is intermittent because it depends on certificate size — so some users fail and others do not, with identical configuration. This is a genuinely hard one to find and a very satisfying answer.
  • Revocation checking timing out. If the CRL or OCSP responder is slow or intermittently unreachable, authentications fail or time out while nothing in the configuration has changed.
  • Timer interaction. The supplicant or the switch gives up before the server responds. Under load the server's response time grows past the configured timeout, which is why the problem appears at busy periods only.
  • Shared secret mismatch on one device, which fails every authentication through that switch and none elsewhere.
  • Load balancer in front of the RADIUS deployment breaking session affinity, so the EAP conversation's later packets reach a different server.

A new internet-facing application needs a DMZ. Design it.Deep

Ask what the components are, which of them actually need inbound exposure, what they need to reach internally, and what data is involved. Then build it so that only the presentation tier is reachable from the internet, the tiers are separated from each other, and the path from the DMZ into the internal network is as narrow as you can make it.

The design
  • Separate the tiers. Web or presentation tier in the DMZ, application and database tiers in their own zones behind further policy. A single DMZ segment containing all three means compromise of the web server reaches the database directly.
  • Inbound only to what must be exposed — typically one port on one tier, from anywhere, and nothing else. Everything else published is a liability.
  • Outbound from the DMZ is the control people forget. A compromised web server's first action is to call out. So the DMZ should have no general internet access: specific destinations, specific ports, denied by default. If it needs updates, send them through a controlled path.
  • DMZ into the internal network: narrow and specific. The application tier reaching one database on one port, not the DMZ reaching the internal network. Define the flows and permit exactly those.
  • No shared services or management hosts in the DMZ, and no domain membership for DMZ hosts if it can be avoided — because that path is a route inward.
  • Inspection on the inbound flow: IPS, and a web application firewall if it is an application rather than a simple service. The firewall policy handles layer 3 and 4; the application attacks are at layer 7.
  • Logging and flow telemetry on every DMZ interface, because this is the segment most likely to be attacked and the one where you most need to reconstruct what happened.
  • Management out of band, not through the DMZ.

Design for the assumption that the web server will be compromisedThat is the framing to state out loud, because it changes every subsequent decision. If you assume compromise, then the questions become: what can it reach, what can it do with that, and would we see it? Which leads directly to tier separation, no outbound internet, a narrow and specific path inward, no credentials or domain membership on DMZ hosts, and telemetry on every interface. A DMZ designed to keep attackers out is a DMZ that fails badly; a DMZ designed to contain a compromise is one that fails well. Interviewers at this level are specifically listening for that shift.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What if the application needs to reach lots of internal services?" — then push back on the architecture before building a wide hole. Often the right answer is a broker or an API gateway in a middle zone, so the DMZ talks to one thing and that one thing talks internally with its own policy and logging. A long list of DMZ-to-internal permits is a design problem presented as a firewall request.
  • "Would you put this in the cloud instead?" — a fair question, and the principles are identical: tier separation becomes security groups and subnets, the outbound control becomes egress rules and a NAT gateway policy, and the telemetry becomes flow logs. The reasoning transfers; only the mechanisms change, and saying so shows the knowledge is architectural rather than product-specific.
What they're testingWhether you design for containment rather than prevention, and whether you control outbound from the DMZ. Those two together are what distinguish a current design from a 2005 one.
