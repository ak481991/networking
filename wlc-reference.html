01Architecture

A lightweight AP is not a small wireless router. It is half of a radio system whose other half lives on the controller, and knowing which half does what explains most of wireless troubleshooting.

Split MAC

802.11 MAC functions are divided between the AP and the WLC. The AP keeps everything that must happen in microseconds; the WLC takes everything that can tolerate a round trip.

Stays on the AP (real-time)Moves to the WLC (non-real-time)
Beacons and probe responsesAssociation and re-association processing
Packet acknowledgement and retransmission802.1X / EAP authentication relay to RADIUS
Frame queuing and prioritisationKey management and distribution
MAC-layer encryption and decryptionClient association database and mobility
Data rate selection, RTS/CTSRRM decisions: channel, power
Collecting RF measurementsSecurity policy, rogue classification, QoS policy
Why this matters when troubleshootingIf a client cannot see the SSID or gets no probe response, the problem is at the AP or in RF. If it associates but fails authentication or never gets policy applied, the problem is on the controller or beyond it. The split tells you which half to look at first.

03CAPWAP

The tunnel between AP and controller. Two UDP ports, one of them always encrypted, and an MTU problem waiting to happen.

The two channels

ChannelPortCarriesEncryption
ControlUDP 5246Join, configuration, keepalives, client state, RRM dataDTLS, always
DataUDP 5247Client traffic, in local mode onlyDTLS optional, off by default

Legacy LWAPP used UDP 12222 for data and 12223 for control. You will still see those ports in old firewall rules and old documentation.

The AP always initiates. The controller never dials out to an AP, which means the AP must be able to reach the WLC management address, but not the reverse.

MTU and fragmentation

CAPWAP adds overhead to every encapsulated frame — outer IP, UDP, the CAPWAP header, and in local mode the original 802.11 or Ethernet frame inside. A full-size client packet plus CAPWAP overhead exceeds 1500 bytes and must be fragmented.

Blocked fragments is the classic silent failureIf anything in the path drops IP fragments or blocks ICMP "fragmentation needed", the AP joins fine (small control packets) and then client traffic breaks for larger frames, or the AP joins and drops repeatedly during image download. Symptoms look nothing like an MTU problem until you test for it.
! From the AP CLI — test the real path
AP# ping 10.50.0.10 size 1500 df-bit

DTLS and certificates

The control channel is protected by DTLS, authenticated with certificates on both ends. Three certificate types appear on APs:

TypeMeaning
MIC — Manufacturer Installed CertificateBurned in at the factory. What almost every modern AP uses
SSC — Self-Signed CertificateOlder APs, or field-converted units
LSC — Locally Significant CertificateIssued by your own PKI, for environments that require it
Certificate validity makes the clock load-bearingMIC certificates have a validity window. If the controller's clock is wrong — after a power loss with no NTP, say — it rejects every AP certificate as not-yet-valid or expired, and no AP joins. The log says the certificate failed, which sends people hunting for a PKI problem rather than checking the time. Scenario 01.

The AP sends a CAPWAP echo at the heartbeat interval. Missing a configurable number of them tears the tunnel down and the AP restarts discovery. On a lossy WAN, raising the retransmit count prevents a brief loss event from causing a full rejoin and the outage that goes with it.

Off-channel scanning

A local-mode AP leaves its serving channel briefly to scan others — roughly 50 ms every 180 seconds per channel by default. That is how it finds rogues and feeds RRM. The cost is a short gap in service, usually harmless but occasionally visible to sensitive real-time traffic.

! Monitor mode changes what gets scanned and how often
ap monitor-mode fast-channel-scan
show ap name AP-CAMPUS-01 config dot11 5ghz | include Channel Scan

If you need full-time scanning without the service gap, dedicate an AP in Monitor mode rather than tuning local-mode scanning aggressively.

05AP join process

Four phases. An AP that will not join has failed at exactly one of them, and knowing which narrows the cause to two or three possibilities.

AP join · four phases, with the usual failure cause of each.

How an AP finds a controller

The AP tries these in order and builds a candidate list from everything that answers:

  1. Previously joined controllers — stored in NVRAM, including the primary, secondary and tertiary names it was given.
  2. DHCP option 43 — one or more WLC management addresses handed out with the lease.
  3. DNS — resolves CISCO-CAPWAP-CONTROLLER.<local-domain>, with the domain coming from DHCP option 15.
  4. Layer 3 broadcast — a subnet broadcast discovery, which only reaches a WLC on the same subnet.
  5. Statically configured — primed over the AP console.
  6. Mobility group members — a controller that answers will share its mobility peers as further candidates.

Having found candidates, the AP picks one: a configured primary, secondary or tertiary first; then a controller configured as master; then, failing both, the least loaded controller by AP count relative to capacity.

Priming an AP from the console beats guessingWhen discovery is the problem, connect to the AP console and set the controller directly. If it then joins, discovery was the issue; if it still fails, the problem is further along. That single test splits the problem space in half.
AP# capwap ap controller ip address 10.50.0.10
AP# capwap ap primary-base WLC-01 10.50.0.10
AP# capwap ap hostname AP-BR-01
AP# show capwap client rcb
AP# show capwap client ha

DHCP option 43

The most common discovery method, and the most commonly mis-encoded. For Cisco APs it is a TLV: type f1, length = 4 × number of controllers, then each management IP in hex.

! One controller at 10.50.0.10
!   10 = 0a, 50 = 32, 0 = 00, 10 = 0a   ->  0a32000a
!   type f1, length 04

ip dhcp pool AP-MGMT
 network 10.50.0.0 255.255.255.0
 default-router 10.50.0.1
 option 43 hex f1040a32000a
 option 60 ascii "Cisco AP c9130"

! Two controllers, 10.50.0.10 and 10.50.0.11
!   length 08, then both addresses
 option 43 hex f1080a32000a0a32000b
Getting the length byte wrong is the usual errorThe length is the number of bytes of address data, not the number of controllers. One controller is 04, two is 08, three is 0c. A wrong length makes the AP discard the option silently — no log, no error, it simply falls through to DNS and then fails. Decode your own hex before blaming anything else.

On a Windows DHCP server the same value is entered as a hex string on the scope option, and on Infoblox or similar it is usually entered as a vendor-encapsulated option. The encoding is identical; only the entry form differs.

06Switch configuration

The switchport config differs completely between local mode and FlexConnect. Getting it wrong is a top-three cause of wireless problems that look like RF problems.

Local-mode AP port — access

All client traffic is inside CAPWAP, so the AP needs exactly one VLAN: its own management VLAN.

interface GigabitEthernet1/0/10
 description AP-CAMPUS-01
 switchport mode access
 switchport access vlan 50
 switchport nonegotiate
 spanning-tree portfast
 spanning-tree bpduguard enable
 power inline auto
 ! trust the AP's markings — it copies client DSCP to the CAPWAP header
 trust device cisco-ap
 no shutdown

FlexConnect AP port — trunk

Client traffic is bridged locally, so every client VLAN must reach the AP. The AP management VLAN becomes the native VLAN.

interface GigabitEthernet1/0/10
 description AP-BRANCH-01 FlexConnect
 switchport mode trunk
 switchport trunk native vlan 60
 switchport trunk allowed vlan 60,61,62
 switchport nonegotiate
 spanning-tree portfast trunk
 spanning-tree bpduguard enable
 power inline auto
 trust device cisco-ap
 no shutdown
The native VLAN must match the AP's FlexConnect native VLAN settingIf the switch native VLAN is 60 and the AP's FlexConnect profile says native VLAN 1, the AP's own management traffic is tagged when the switch expects it untagged. The AP never joins, or joins and drops. Both ends must agree, and the mapping lives in the flex profile on the controller.

Other switch-side things that matter

  • IGMP snooping — needed on client VLANs if you run multicast applications or mDNS discovery.
  • DHCP snooping — if enabled, trust the uplink toward the DHCP server or client leases fail.
  • Storm control — a broadcast storm on a client VLAN will saturate every AP on it.
  • Port security — do not use it on an AP port. Many client MACs appear behind one port in FlexConnect.
  • 802.1X on the AP port — supported, but adds a dependency; the AP must authenticate before it can reach the WLC.

07The 9800 tag model

The biggest conceptual change from AireOS. Nothing is applied to an AP directly — everything arrives through three tags.

Three tags, and what each carries

TagContainsAnswers
Policy tagWLAN profile + policy profile pairsWhich SSIDs does this AP broadcast, and what policy applies to clients on them?
Site tagAP join profile, flex profile, local-site flagIs this AP local mode or FlexConnect, and what AP-level settings apply?
RF tagRF profiles for 2.4, 5 and 6 GHzWhat radio settings apply — channels, power, data rates?

Every AP has exactly one of each. Change a tag and every AP carrying it changes with it, which is both the power and the hazard of the model.

The split that trips people up

A WLAN is defined in two objects, not one:

  • WLAN profile — the over-the-air part: SSID name, security, 802.11 settings, PMF.
  • Policy profile — the wired-side part: VLAN, central or local switching, ACLs, QoS, AAA override, session timeout.

The policy tag is what binds a WLAN profile to a policy profile. On AireOS all of this lived on the WLAN itself, which is why AireOS engineers go looking for the VLAN setting on the WLAN and cannot find it.

08Client join flow

What actually happens between a laptop and the network. The 802.11 part is identical for every security type; only what follows association differs.

Client join · WPA2-Enterprise, probe through to RUN state.

Phase 1 — discovery and association

  1. Scanning. Passive: the client listens for beacons, sent every 102.4 ms by default. Active: the client sends probe requests and collects responses.
  2. 802.11 Authentication. A two-frame Open System exchange. Despite the name it authenticates nothing — it is a legacy handshake retained for compatibility. Shared Key authentication (WEP) is long dead.
  3. Association. The client sends an Association Request listing its capabilities and chosen security suite; the AP replies with an Association Response and an AID (association identifier).

At this point the client is associated but has no data access. The controller holds it in a pre-RUN state until security and addressing complete.

If a client fails before association, it is RF or capabilityAssociation failures are about what the client and AP can agree on: a data rate the client cannot do, a security suite it does not support, a band it has no radio for, or simply too weak a signal. Nothing on the controller's policy side has been consulted yet.

09Keys and the 4-way handshake

The key hierarchy

802.1X:   EAP method  ->  MSK (64 bytes)  ->  PMK = first 32 bytes
PSK:      passphrase + SSID  ->  PBKDF2  ->  PMK directly
SAE:      Dragonfly exchange  ->  PMK

PMK + ANonce + SNonce + AP MAC + client MAC  ->  PTK

PTK splits into:
  KCK  16 bytes   key confirmation — MICs on handshake frames
  KEK  16 bytes   key encryption — protects the GTK in transit
  TK   16 bytes   temporal key — encrypts actual unicast data

The PMK is never transmitted. Both sides derive it independently — the client from the EAP method or passphrase, the controller from the RADIUS Access-Accept. The 4-way handshake then proves both ends really do hold the same PMK, without ever sending it.

The four messages

MsgDirectionCarriesPurpose
M1AP → ClientANonceGives the client what it needs to compute the PTK
M2Client → APSNonce + MICAP can now compute the PTK; the MIC proves the client has the right PMK
M3AP → ClientGTK (encrypted with KEK) + MIC + install flagDelivers the group key and proves the AP's PMK
M4Client → APACKConfirms installation. Both sides start encrypting

The GTK is shared by every client on that AP and encrypts broadcast and multicast. It is rotated when a client leaves, if GTK randomisation is enabled.

M1 sent, no M2 back, repeatedly — the PSK is wrongThis is the signature of a mismatched passphrase. The client computes a PTK from the wrong PMK, its MIC fails, and the AP retries M1 until it gives up. In a capture you see M1 four times and nothing else. For 802.1X the same pattern means the MSK did not match, usually a PMKID caching or fast-roaming problem.

PMF — 802.11w

Protected Management Frames cryptographically protect deauthentication and disassociation frames, which are otherwise unauthenticated and trivially spoofed. Without PMF, anyone can knock any client off any network with a deauth flood.

SettingBehaviour
disabledNo protection. Legacy only
optionalClients that support it use it; others still connect
requiredClients without PMF cannot associate
 
wlan CORP-WLAN 1 CORP
 security pmf optional
 ! WPA3 requires it
 security wpa wpa3
 security pmf mandatory
Start at optionalSetting PMF to required on an existing SSID silently excludes every older client — IoT devices, medical equipment, older printers. The symptom is "some devices stopped connecting after the change" with no useful log on the client side. Run optional, check show wireless client summary for which clients negotiated it, then tighten.

802.11r is the right answer for voice. It brings roam time down to the tens of milliseconds a call needs, whereas a full reauthentication can take hundreds and will be audible.

If devices stop connecting right after you enabled fast roaming, this is why.

802.11k and 802.11v

  • 802.11k — neighbour reports. The AP tells the client which other APs are nearby and on which channels, so the client does not have to scan the whole band to find a roam target.
  • 802.11v — BSS Transition Management. The network can suggest a client move to a better AP, and with the disassociation imminent flag, apply real pressure.

Neither forces anything. A client is always free to ignore both, and many do. Together they are the best available answer to sticky clients, but they are a nudge rather than a control.

Sticky clients

A client that holds on to a distant AP at −80 dBm while standing under one at −45. The client's roaming algorithm is the cause and you cannot change it, but you can change the environment it decides in:

  • Disable low data rates. If 1, 2, 5.5 and 11 Mbps are disabled, a far AP becomes unusable sooner and the client is forced to look elsewhere. The single most effective lever.
  • Reduce transmit power so cells are smaller and the signal gradient steeper.
  • Enable 802.11k and v to inform and nudge.

12RF fundamentals

Four numbers decide whether wireless works: signal strength, noise floor, the gap between them, and how busy the channel is. Everything else is detail.

Signal, noise and SNR · the gap is what determines usable data rate.

RSSI

Received Signal Strength Indicator, in dBm — decibels relative to one milliwatt. Always negative in Wi-Fi, and closer to zero is stronger.

RSSIQualitySupports
-30 to -50ExcellentEverything. Usually means you are standing under the AP
-50 to -60Very goodHigh data rates, voice, video
-60 to -67GoodVoice design target
-67 to -70FairData. Marginal for voice
-70 to -80PoorLow rates, retries, poor experience
below -80UnusableAssociation may hold but nothing works well
The decibel shortcuts worth memorising+3 dB is double the power, −3 dB is half. +10 dB is ten times. So −60 dBm is ten times the power of −70 dBm, and −67 dBm is roughly half of −64. This makes coverage maths quick: adding 3 dB of antenna gain doubles effective radiated power.

Noise floor

The background RF energy on a channel with no Wi-Fi transmission in progress. A clean environment sits around −92 to −95 dBm. Anything above roughly −85 dBm means something is injecting energy.

Raised noise comes from non-Wi-Fi sources — microwave ovens, cordless phones, video senders, Bluetooth, wireless cameras, bad power supplies, faulty fluorescent ballasts. Other Wi-Fi networks mostly show as channel utilisation rather than noise, because the radio demodulates them as valid frames.

show ap dot11 5ghz summary
show ap auto-rf dot11 5ghz
show ap name AP-CAMPUS-01 auto-rf dot11 5ghz | include Noise|Interference|Utilization

SNR

Signal minus noise floor, expressed in dB. This is what determines the modulation and coding scheme the radio can use, and therefore the actual throughput.

SNR = RSSI - noise floor

  -55 dBm signal, -92 dBm noise  ->  37 dB SNR   excellent
  -70 dBm signal, -92 dBm noise  ->  22 dB SNR   data only
  -70 dBm signal, -80 dBm noise  ->  10 dB SNR   barely functional
SNRSupports
40 dB+Maximum rates, highest MCS
25 dBVoice minimum
20 dBData minimum for a reasonable experience
15 dBLow rates only, high retry count
below 10 dBEffectively unusable
Good RSSI with bad performance means the noise floorThe third example above is the one that confuses people. The client reports a perfectly acceptable −70 dBm, so coverage looks fine on any heat map, but with a noise floor of −80 the usable gap is only 10 dB. Always read RSSI and noise together — neither number means anything alone.

Channel utilisation

The percentage of airtime already in use. Because Wi-Fi is a shared half-duplex medium with contention, a busy channel degrades everyone regardless of their signal quality.

UtilisationMeaning
under 30%Healthy
30–50%Busy but workable
50–70%Contention noticeable. Voice suffers
over 70%Saturated. Add capacity or reduce cell size

High utilisation with few clients usually means legacy slow clients consuming airtime, or co-channel interference from your own APs. Both are design problems rather than capacity problems.

Transmit power and EIRP

EIRP = transmit power + antenna gain - cable loss

  17 dBm radio + 6 dBi antenna - 1 dB cable  =  22 dBm EIRP

Regulators cap EIRP, not radio power, so a high-gain antenna requires a corresponding reduction in transmit power. Controllers enforce this per regulatory domain automatically.

Turning power up is usually the wrong fixIt extends the cell, which increases co-channel interference with your own APs and makes sticky clients worse. It also creates an asymmetry: the AP shouts further than the client can answer, so the client associates at a distance from which it cannot be heard. Match AP power roughly to client power — most laptops transmit at 15–17 dBm, phones often less.

14RRM

Radio Resource Management picks channels and power automatically. It works well by default and badly when people tune it without understanding the inputs.

RF groups and the leader

APs that hear each other belong to one RF group; their controllers elect an RF group leader which runs the calculation for all of them. Without a common leader, each controller optimises in isolation and the results conflict.

show ap dot11 5ghz group
show ap dot11 24ghz group

! RF group name must match across controllers
wireless rf-network CAMPUS-RF
Split RF groups produce channel thrashTwo controllers each believing they lead will reassign channels against each other indefinitely. Symptom: APs changing channel every DCA interval, and clients dropping each time. Confirm one leader with show ap dot11 5ghz group on every controller — they must all name the same one.

The four algorithms

AlgorithmDecidesDefault interval
DCA — Dynamic Channel AssignmentWhich channel each radio uses10 minutes, with change thresholds
TPC — Transmit Power ControlPower level, aiming for a target neighbour RSSI10 minutes
CHDM — Coverage Hole DetectionRaises power where clients report poor RSSI180 seconds
FRA — Flexible Radio AssignmentWhether a dual-band radio serves 2.4, 5, or monitorsHourly
 
show ap dot11 5ghz rrm dca
show ap dot11 5ghz rrm txpower
show ap auto-rf dot11 5ghz
show ap dot11 5ghz rrm coverage

! TPC target — how strongly APs should hear their third neighbour
ap dot11 5ghz rrm tpc-threshold -70

! DCA sensitivity: low = change rarely, high = change readily
ap dot11 5ghz rrm channel dca sensitivity low
The TPC threshold is the main coverage leverIt sets how loudly each AP should hear its third-loudest neighbour. A value nearer −60 shrinks cells and suits high density; nearer −80 grows them and suits sparse coverage. Changing this is almost always better than setting fixed power levels per AP, which stops RRM adapting at all.

RF profiles

Per-band settings applied through the RF tag, so different site types can have different radio behaviour on one controller.

ap dot11 5ghz rf-profile HIGH-DENSITY
 channel width 20
 tx-power-max 14
 tx-power-min 5

 rate RATE_12M mandatory

 coverage voice rssi threshold -70

!
wireless tag rf OFFICE-RF-TAG

show ap rf-profile summary
show ap rf-profile name HIGH-DENSITY detail

Disabling the lowest data rates in a high-density profile is the single most effective change: it shrinks effective cell size, forces earlier roaming, and stops distant clients consuming disproportionate airtime at 6 Mbps.

When to override RRM

  • Static channels for a known-problem area, or where a DFS channel must be avoided.
  • Static power only when the physical environment makes the automatic result wrong — an AP in a lift lobby, say.
  • Exclude channels from DCA rather than fixing channels manually, so RRM still optimises what remains.
A manually fixed radio is invisible to RRM's optimisationIts neighbours will still be adjusted around it, but it will never adapt to a change they make. A handful of fixed radios in an otherwise automatic deployment is a common cause of persistent, unexplained interference. Document every override and review them.

17Real-world scenarios

Ten incidents on the lab topology, from reported symptom to fix. Work out the cause before expanding the diagnosis.

No AP will join after a power outage
Reported

Site lost power overnight. Everything came back, switches are fine, APs have IP addresses and are reachable by ping. Not one AP has joined the controller.

What you see 
WLC# show ap summary
Number of APs: 0

WLC# show wireless stats ap join summary
AP Name   Ethernet MAC    Status      Last Failure Reason
AP-C-01   f4db.e6aa.0001  Not joined  DTLS handshake failure
AP-C-02   f4db.e6aa.0002  Not joined  DTLS handshake failure

Every AP, same failure, all at once. That pattern points at something global on the controller rather than anything per-AP.

Diagnosis 
AP# debug capwap client error
CAPWAP: DTLS handshake failed
CAPWAP: Certificate verification failed: certificate is not yet valid

WLC# show clock
*00:14:22.391 UTC Mon Jan 1 2001

WLC# show ntp associations
%NTP is not enabled.

The controller lost its clock during the outage and came back at the epoch default. AP manufacturer-installed certificates carry a validity window; against a 2001 clock every one of them is "not yet valid", so DTLS fails for all APs identically.

Root cause

No NTP configured, and the hardware clock did not survive the outage. The certificates and the APs were never the problem.

Fix 
WLC(config)# clock timezone IST 5 30
WLC(config)# ntp server 10.50.1.5
WLC(config)# ntp server 10.50.1.6
WLC# clock set 09:20:00 20 Nov 2026        ! immediate, while NTP syncs

WLC# show clock
WLC# show ntp status

APs begin joining within a couple of minutes as their retry timers fire.

TakeawayEvery AP failing identically and simultaneously is a controller-wide cause, not an AP cause. Certificate errors after any power event mean check the clock first — the message says certificate, the problem is time. Configure NTP on controllers as a build standard, with at least two sources.
Branch APs join, then reboot, forever
Reported

New branch over a DMVPN tunnel. APs get addresses, discover the controller, start to join, then reboot. The cycle repeats every few minutes. Campus APs on the same controller are fine.

What you see

AP-BR-01   ...  Downloading
! two minutes later, gone from the list entirely

Control traffic works — discovery, DTLS and join all succeed. It fails at the image download, which is the first thing that sends large packets.

Diagnosis

AP# ping 10.50.0.10 size 1500 df-bit
.....

AP# ping 10.50.0.10 size 1350 df-bit

Small packets pass, large ones do not. The DMVPN tunnel plus IPsec overhead leaves well under 1500 bytes of usable payload, and the path is dropping the fragments rather than signalling back.

Root cause

Path MTU reduced by tunnel overhead, with ICMP fragmentation-needed filtered somewhere upstream so path MTU discovery cannot work. The CAPWAP control channel is small enough to survive; the image transfer is not.

Fix 
! Reduce the AP's CAPWAP MTU for this site
WLC(config)# ap profile BRANCH-PROFILE
WLC(config-ap-profile)# capwap-mtu 1300

! And fix the underlying path — on the branch router
interface Tunnel0

 ip tcp adjust-mss 1360

Predownloading the image during a window also avoids the loop while the MTU fix is deployed.

TakeawayAn AP that joins and then fails during image download is an MTU problem almost every time, because the download is the first large transfer. Test with ping size 1500 df-bit from the AP itself — not from a router nearby, since the AP's path may differ. Small-packet reachability proves nothing.

Reported

A new SSID was added at the branch. Clients connect — the laptop shows connected — but get a 169.254 address and no network access. The same SSID works on campus.

Wi-Fi is slow, and the signal is excellent
Reported

Open-plan office. Users report slow Wi-Fi all afternoon. Signal bars are full. The heat map shows −55 dBm or better everywhere.

What you see 
WLC# show ap name AP-CAMPUS-07 auto-rf dot11 5ghz | include Util|Noise|Clients
 Channel Utilization................... 82%
 Noise Floor.......................... -91 dBm
 Number of Clients.................... 31

Signal and noise are both fine. The channel is 82% occupied — the medium is busy, so everyone waits regardless of how strong their signal is.

Diagnosis 
WLC# show ap dot11 5ghz summary
AP Name        Channel  Width  TxPwr   Clients
AP-CAMPUS-05   36       80     1 (23dBm)   28
AP-CAMPUS-06   36       80     1 (23dBm)   34
AP-CAMPUS-07   36       80     1 (23dBm)   31
AP-CAMPUS-08   149      80     1 (23dBm)   12

Three adjacent APs on the same channel at maximum power. With 80 MHz channels there are only six non-overlapping options in 5 GHz, and DCA could not find a clean assignment. The APs are shouting over each other — textbook co-channel interference.

WLC# show ap auto-rf dot11 5ghz | include Co-Channel
 Co-Channel Interference.............. -61 dBm
! neighbours heard at -61 dBm on the same channel is very loud
Root cause

80 MHz channel width in a dense deployment, combined with maximum transmit power. Both choices were made to "improve performance" and did the opposite: wide channels removed the reuse options, and high power made every AP audible to every other.

Fix 
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rrm channel dca chan-width 20
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rrm tpc-threshold -65

! and disable the low rates to shrink effective cell size
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rate RATE_6M disable
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rate RATE_9M disable
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rate RATE_12M mandatory

! let DCA re-run
WLC# ap dot11 5ghz rrm channel dca restart
WLC# show ap name AP-CAMPUS-07 auto-rf dot11 5ghz | include Util
 Channel Utilization................... 34%
TakeawayFull signal bars say nothing about capacity. In high density, 20 MHz channels deliver more aggregate throughput than 80 MHz, because channel reuse matters more than per-client peak rate. Check utilisation and co-channel interference before anyone suggests adding APs — more APs on the same wide channels makes it worse.

Reported

Wi-Fi handsets drop or go silent for two to three seconds while users walk. Stationary calls are perfect. Coverage survey shows no gaps.

What you see

Time            AP Name          Type       RSSI
09:14:02        AP-F2-04         Associate  -58
09:16:41        AP-F2-09         Associate   -71
09:19:03        AP-F3-02         Associate   -73

! "Associate", not "Roam" — each move is a full reconnection
Diagnosis 
WLC# show wireless client mac-address aaaa.bbbb.cccc detail | include 11r|11k|11v|Key
       Fast Roam : Not supported
          11k/11v : Disabled
   Key Management : WPA2 802.1X (full)

WLC# show run | section wlan VOICE-WLAN
wlan VOICE-WLAN 2 VOICE
 security dot1x authentication-list ISE-AUTH
 security wpa wpa2 ciphers aes
! no FT, no 11k, no 11v

Every roam is a complete 802.1X reauthentication to ISE — EAP exchange plus RADIUS round trips plus a full 4-way handshake. That takes hundreds of milliseconds and is plainly audible on a call. It is also why the handsets cling on until the signal is poor: with no neighbour report, they only scan when things get bad.

WLC# show ap dot11 5ghz rate | include RATE_6M|RATE_12M
 RATE_6M .... mandatory
! lowest rate still enabled, so a far AP stays "good enough"
Root cause

No fast-roaming method enabled on the voice SSID, no 802.11k neighbour reports to guide the client, and low data rates keeping distant APs viable so the handset roams late and at poor RSSI.

Fix 
WLC(config)# wlan VOICE-WLAN 2 VOICE
WLC(config-wlan)# shutdown
WLC(config-wlan)# security ft adaptive
WLC(config-wlan)# dot11k neighbor-list
WLC(config-wlan)# dot11v BSS-transition
WLC(config-wlan)# no shutdown

! make distant APs genuinely unattractive
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rate RATE_6M disable
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rate RATE_9M disable
WLC(config)# ap dot11 5ghz rate RATE_12M mandatory
WLC# show wireless client mac-address aaaa.bbbb.cccc mobility history
09:42:11        AP-F2-09   FT Roam   -62
09:44:55        AP-F3-02   FT Roam   -59
TakeawayA coverage survey measures where signal exists, not how well clients move between cells. For voice you need 802.11r so the roam is cheap, 802.11k so the client knows where to go, and disabled low data rates so it goes before the call degrades. Use mobility history to tell a real roam from a full reassociation — the word in that column is the diagnosis.

A few devices stopped connecting after a security change
Reported

WPA3 was enabled on the corporate SSID over the weekend. Most laptops are fine. Barcode scanners, a few printers and some older tablets no longer connect at all. They show "cannot connect to network" with no detail.

What you see 
WLC# show wireless client summary | count
Number of clients: 412        ! was 460

WLC# show logging profile wireless last 1 hours filter mac 0024.d7xx.xxxx
... Association request from 0024.d7xx.xxxx
... Rejecting association: RSN IE mismatch — PMF required, client does not support
Diagnosis 
WLC# show run | section wlan CORP-WLAN
wlan CORP-WLAN 1 CORP
 security wpa wpa3
 security wpa akm sae
 security pmf mandatory

WPA3 mandates Protected Management Frames. Clients without 802.11w support cannot negotiate the RSN information element and are rejected at association — before any authentication, which is why nothing useful appears in the client's own logs.

! which clients negotiated PMF before the change?
WLC# show wireless client summary | include PMF
Root cause

A hard cutover to WPA3 on an SSID carrying a mixed device population, including embedded and industrial devices that will never support PMF.

Fix — two options 
! A. Transition mode — WPA2 and WPA3 on one SSID
WLC(config)# wlan CORP-WLAN 1 CORP
WLC(config-wlan)# shutdown
WLC(config-wlan)# security wpa wpa2
WLC(config-wlan)# security wpa wpa3
WLC(config-wlan)# security wpa akm dot1x
WLC(config-wlan)# security wpa akm sae
WLC(config-wlan)# security pmf optional
WLC(config-wlan)# no shutdown

! B. Separate SSID for legacy devices, on its own VLAN
!    with tighter network-side controls to compensate

Option B is better security practice where the legacy devices can be segmented. Option A is the fast recovery.

TakeawayClients rejected at association fail before authentication, so neither the client nor the RADIUS logs explain anything — only the controller's association reject does. Before any security tightening, inventory what actually connects: show wireless client summary with PMF and capability columns tells you who will survive. Move to optional first, measure, then make it mandatory.

A single AP in a conference room loses its 5 GHz radio several times a day. 2.4 GHz stays up. The AP itself never leaves the controller. Replacing the AP did not help.

The switch's PoE budget is exhausted. This port negotiated down to 15.4 W, and the AP sheds load by shutting a radio when it cannot sustain both. It is the last port added to a fully loaded switch.

 PoE Pre-Standard Switch............ Disabled

Root cause

Switch PoE budget exceeded. The AP received only 802.3af power and disabled its 5 GHz radio to stay within it. No alarm fired because nothing failed — the AP did exactly what it is designed to do.

! Immediate — prioritise this port over something less critical
SW(config)# interface GigabitEthernet1/0/22
SW(config-if)# power inline port priority high

SW(config)# interface GigabitEthernet1/0/40
SW(config-if)# power inline maximum 7000

! Real fix — add a power supply or redistribute APs
SW# show power inline module 1
SW# show env power
TakeawayAn AP in degraded power mode disables radios, drops spatial streams or reduces transmit power, usually without raising an alarm — and it looks exactly like an RF or hardware problem. Check show power inline ... detail and the module budget before investigating anything radio-related, especially on a switch where APs have been added over time.
