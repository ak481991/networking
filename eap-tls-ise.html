01The three-party model

EAP-TLS is a mutual-certificate TLS handshake between the endpoint and ISE. The switch or WLC between them never sees inside it, and that single fact explains most of what follows.

The three roles IEEE 802.1X

  • Supplicant — software on the endpoint (Windows native, Cisco Secure Client NAM, wpa_supplicant, an iOS or macOS profile). Holds the client certificate and, critically, the matching private key.
  • Authenticator / NAD — the access switch or WLC. Owns the port state machine, holds the RADIUS shared secret, translates between two encapsulations. Nothing more.
  • Authentication server — the ISE Policy Service Node. Terminates TLS, validates the client certificate, resolves an identity, runs the policy sets.

Two transport domains

LegEncapsulationLayerNotes
Supplicant ↔ NADEAPOL (EAP over LAN)L2 frame, EtherType 0x888EWireless carries EAPOL inside 802.11 data frames after association
NAD ↔ ISEEAP over RADIUS (RFC 3579)UDP 1812, legacy 1645EAP packet carried in the EAP-Message attribute, often split across several

The pass-through insight

The NAD lifts the EAP packet out of the EAPOL frame, drops it verbatim into EAP-Message, and does the reverse on the way back. It has no EAP certificate, no trust store, and no part in validating anything. Three consequences worth saying out loud to your team:

  1. If the TLS handshake fails, switch configuration is almost never the cause. Look at supplicant trust and ISE certificates instead.
  2. The NAD does own timers, retransmissions and the port state machine — so timeout-shaped failures are switch-side, even when the handshake itself is fine.
  3. The only identity the NAD ever sees is the clear-text EAP-Response/Identity. It cannot read the certificate, so it cannot make decisions on it.

Why EAP-TLS rather than the alternatives

MethodClient credentialServer validated by clientTypical ISE identity store
EAP-TLSX.509 cert + private keyYes, by certificateCertificate Authentication Profile, optional AD/LDAP lookup
PEAP-MSCHAPv2Password inside a TLS tunnelOnly if the supplicant is configured toActive Directory
EAP-FASTPAC, then an inner methodVia PAC provisioningAD / internal users
TEAP (RFC 7170)Cert and/or password, chainedYes, by certificateBoth, in one session

EAP-TLS is the only one of these where the client credential is a private key that never leaves the endpoint — ideally never leaves the TPM. There is no password to phish, replay or stuff, and the handshake produces strong keying material for MACsec and WPA3-Enterprise as a by-product.

The trade-off is PKI operations: enrolment, renewal, revocation and trust distribution become your problem. Plain EAP-TLS also authenticates one identity per session, which is why TEAP exists for machine-and-user chaining.

Every EAP-TLS message on the access leg is a TLS record inside an EAP packet inside an EAPOL frame. Being able to point at each header in Wireshark is the single most convincing thing you can do in a demo.

2. The EAP packet RFC 3748

Success and Failure packets are four bytes total and carry no Type field — which is exactly why a bare EAP-Success can never be trusted on its own, and why 802.11i added the 4-way handshake.

Two points to stress. First, EAP-Message fragmentation across attributes is a RADIUS-layer mechanic, entirely separate from EAP-TLS fragmentation — a frequent source of confusion. Second, Message-Authenticator is what proves the NAD knows the shared secret; a mismatch shows up in ISE as a dropped request with no authentication log at all, which sends people hunting in the wrong place.

Wireshark filter for the ISE leg: radius, and radius.Calling_Station_Id contains "aabb.ccdd" to pin one endpoint.

03The end-to-end flow

EAP-TLS message flow · 3 parties, 14 messages. Squares on the middle lifeline mark a relayed message.

Reading the ladder

The small squares on the middle lifeline mark where the switch relays a message it cannot read: it moves the EAP packet between the EAPOL frame and the EAP-Message attribute and nothing else. Only the first phase and the last are the switch's own business.

Two details that are easy to miss. The dashed row is not one message — it stands for an acknowledgement after every fragment, so a handshake split into six fragments costs six extra round trips. And the Access-Accept carries three separate things at once: the EAP-Success the supplicant is waiting for, the session keys the switch will use, and the authorisation attributes that decide what the endpoint can reach.

04Inside the tunnel: the TLS 1.2 handshake

This is the part your colleagues will care about, because in TLS 1.2 almost all of it is visible in clear text on the wire. You can literally point at both certificates in Wireshark.

Message by message
#DirectionTLS messageWhat it establishes
1Client → ISEClientHelloHighest TLS version offered, 32-byte client random, cipher suite list, extensions: supported_groups, signature_algorithms, ec_point_formats, optionally a session ticket
2ISE → ClientServerHelloChosen version and one cipher suite, 32-byte server random, session ID
3ISE → ClientCertificateISE's EAP certificate plus intermediates. Visible in the clear — what the supplicant evaluates for trust
4ISE → ClientServerKeyExchangeECDHE/DHE parameters signed with the EAP cert's private key. Proves ISE holds the key, gives forward secrecy
5ISE → ClientCertificateRequestThe demand for a client cert, plus the list of CA distinguished names ISE accepts. The field that bloats and causes fragmentation pain
6ISE → ClientServerHelloDoneEnd of the server's first flight
7Client → ISECertificateClient cert and chain, also in the clear. If the endpoint sends a leaf with no intermediate, this is where you see it
8Client → ISEClientKeyExchangeClient's ECDHE public value. Both sides can now compute the pre-master secret
9Client → ISECertificateVerifyA signature over every handshake message so far, made with the client's private key. The actual proof of possession
10Client → ISEChangeCipherSpecSwitch to the negotiated keys
11Client → ISEFinishedEncrypted MAC over the handshake transcript. Detects tampering
12ISE → ClientChangeCipherSpec, FinishedServer does the same. Handshake complete

What each half actually proves

Worth separating explicitly, because people conflate them:

  • ISE proves its identity with messages 3 and 4. The certificate asserts a name; the signature in ServerKeyExchange proves ISE holds the private key for it.
  • The client proves its identity with messages 7 and 9. Same split: the certificate asserts, CertificateVerify proves.
  • Neither side has authorised anything yet. Authorisation happens after the handshake, when ISE maps the certificate to an identity and runs policy.
Demo momentAsk the room: if an attacker copies a client certificate off a laptop, can they authenticate? No — without the private key they cannot produce a valid CertificateVerify. That is the whole argument for non-exportable keys in the TPM.

Cipher and version negotiation in ISE

ISE constrains what it accepts under Administration → System → Settings → Security Settings:

  • Allowed TLS versions — disabling TLS 1.0/1.1 is a common hardening step that breaks older printers, phones and medical devices. Expect this when a specific device class stops authenticating after a hardening change.
  • Allow SHA-1 ciphers and Allow ECDHE-RSA ciphers — both gate EAP authentication specifically; the ECDHE-RSA option covers the workflow where ISE acts as an EAP server.
  • FIPS mode narrows the suite list further and forbids several key sizes.

ISE supports both RSA and ECDSA server certificates, with secp256r1 and secp384r1 among the supported curves. A cipher-suite mismatch fails very early — ClientHello, then an immediate alert, with no certificates exchanged at all. That early-alert signature is how you tell a cipher problem from a trust problem.

05Fragmentation and round trips

A TLS handshake with two certificate chains is several kilobytes. EAP has no fragmentation of its own, so EAP-TLS (RFC 5216) adds its own scheme in the first byte of Type-Data.

The flags byte

BitNameMeaning when set
7 (0x80)L — Length includedA 4-byte TLS Message Length follows, giving the total size of the reassembled TLS message
6 (0x40)M — More fragmentsMore fragments follow this one
5 (0x20)S — EAP-TLS StartSet only on the first EAP-Request/EAP-TLS from ISE. Carries no TLS data
4–0ReservedMust be zero; TLS 1.3 reuses bit 0 as the version flag

So the opening message from ISE is just 0x20 — a single flags byte, no payload. A first fragment of a large server flight looks like 0xC0 plus a length plus data. Middle fragments are 0x40. The last is 0x00.

The acknowledgement pattern

This is the mechanic people miss. When one side receives a fragment with M set, it must reply with an EAP-TLS Acknowledgement — an EAP packet of type 13 with a flags byte of 0x00 and no data at all. Its only job is to say send the next one.

Each fragment therefore costs a full round trip: NAD to ISE, ISE back to NAD, NAD to supplicant, supplicant back. A server flight split into six fragments is six RADIUS round trips before the client has even started its own flight. This is why EAP-TLS authentication latency scales with certificate chain size, and why it is the method most sensitive to WAN latency between a branch switch and a central PSN.

What drives the fragment count

  1. ISE's EAP fragment size. On the Allowed Protocols object (Policy → Policy Elements → Results → Authentication → Allowed Protocols), default 1002 bytes. Raising it reduces round trips but risks IP fragmentation of the RADIUS datagram; lowering it is the workaround for a path that drops fragmented UDP.
  2. Framed-MTU from the NAD. ISE respects a smaller value if the NAD advertises one.
  3. Certificate chain length and key size. A 4096-bit RSA leaf with two intermediates on both sides is the worst case. ECDSA certificates are dramatically smaller and are the cleanest fix.
  4. The CertificateRequest CA list. ISE includes the DNs of every CA it trusts for client authentication. A trust store with dozens of enabled client-auth CAs makes this message enormous. Pruning Trust for client authentication to only the CAs that actually issue your endpoint certificates is the highest-value tuning knob here, and it is routinely overlooked.

Timer interaction

Fragmentation turns into timeouts when the timers do not allow for the round trips:

WhereSettingGuidance
Switchdot1x timeout tx-periodDefault 30 s. Governs Identity request retries, not the handshake
Switchdot1x timeout supp-timeoutHow long to wait for a supplicant EAP response. Raise for slow endpoints
Switchdot1x max-reauth-reqHow many Identity requests before falling through to MAB
Switchradius-server timeout / retransmitDefaults of 5 s / 3 tries are often too tight for a high-latency PSN path
Switchradius-server deadtime, dead-criteriaControls premature failover to a secondary PSN mid-handshake
Design ruleTLS state is per-PSN. If RADIUS load balancing or a dead-server detection event moves a mid-handshake session to a different PSN, the handshake dies with no useful error. Use stickiness on the RADIUS State attribute or Calling-Station-Id, never round-robin per packet.

06Key derivation

Authentication is only half the output. The handshake also yields keying material, and that is what makes EAP-TLS usable for WPA2/WPA3-Enterprise and MACsec rather than just a gate.

The derivation chain TLS 1.2

  1. ECDHE exchange produces the pre-master secret.
  2. Pre-master secret plus both randoms produce the master secret (48 bytes).
  3. RFC 5216 runs the TLS PRF over the master secret with the ASCII label client EAP encryption and the two randoms, requesting 128 bytes: bytes 0–63 are the MSK, bytes 64–127 the EMSK.

The MSK is the deliverable. Both the supplicant and ISE compute it independently from the handshake they just completed — it is never transmitted between them.

How the MSK reaches the NAD

ISE must hand the MSK to the authenticator, because the authenticator is the device that will actually encrypt traffic. It travels in the Access-Accept as two Microsoft vendor-specific attributes (vendor 311):

AttributeContents
MS-MPPE-Recv-KeyFirst 32 bytes of the MSK
MS-MPPE-Send-KeySecond 32 bytes of the MSK

Both are encrypted using the RADIUS shared secret and the Request-Authenticator. Two consequences:

  • The RADIUS shared secret still matters cryptographically, even with a TLS-based EAP method. A weak shared secret exposes session keys. It is also the strongest argument for RadSec / RADIUS-DTLS.
  • The NAD can read these keys, so the NAD is in your trust boundary for data-plane confidentiality, even though it was blind during the handshake.

Wireless: MSK → PMK → PTK

  1. The first 32 bytes of the MSK become the PMK.
  2. The WLC installs the PMK and runs the 802.11 4-way handshake (EAPOL-Key frames) with the client.
  3. The 4-way handshake mixes the PMK with both MAC addresses and two nonces to produce the PTK, split into KCK, KEK and TK.
  4. The GTK for broadcast and multicast is delivered encrypted inside message 3.

In a wireless capture you will see four EAPOL-Key frames immediately after EAP-Success. If authentication succeeds but the client never passes traffic, the 4-way handshake is where to look — commonly a PMK mismatch after a WLC failover, or a PMKID caching problem.

Wired: MACsec and MKA

For MACsec the MSK becomes the CAK (Connectivity Association Key), which seeds MKA (802.1X-2010). MKA elects a key server and distributes SAKs for the actual frame encryption. Requires EAPOL protocol version 3 and switch support for macsec on the interface. Downlink MACsec with EAP-TLS is the strongest wired posture available, and a natural extension to demo after the basic flow lands.

Session resumption

ISE supports TLS session resumption for EAP-TLS, configured on the Allowed Protocols object with a timeout (often two hours by default). On resumption the client presents a session ticket or session ID, both sides skip the certificate exchange entirely, and the handshake collapses to a few messages.

A genuinely dramatic demo: capture a full authentication, then bounce the port and capture the resumed one. Round-trip count drops sharply and no certificates appear on the wire. It is also an operational caution — a resumed session does not re-validate the certificate, so a revoked certificate can keep working until the resumption cache expires.

07What ISE does with the certificate

A valid handshake is not an authentication. Once the TLS tunnel completes, ISE has a certificate and must turn it into an identity. This is where most real escalations live.

Step 1 — Chain validation

ISE builds a path from the presented leaf to a root in its Trusted Certificates store (Administration → System → Certificates → Trusted Certificates). Each entry has explicit trust purposes, and the relevant one is Trust for client authentication and Syslog. A CA present in the store but without that purpose enabled will fail client authentication while quietly working for other functions — a classic misconfiguration.

ISE also checks:

  • Signature validity up the chain, and basicConstraints CA:TRUE on every issuer
  • notBefore / notAfter on every certificate in the path (clock skew on either side breaks this)
  • Extended Key Usage: clientAuth (1.3.6.1.5.5.7.3.2) on the endpoint certificate, serverAuth (1.3.6.1.5.5.7.3.1) on the ISE EAP certificate
  • Key Usage — digitalSignature at minimum on the client leaf

If the supplicant sends only its leaf and the intermediate is absent from both the chain and the ISE trust store, path building fails. ISE does not fetch intermediates from AIA by default. This is the single most common cause of an unknown-CA failure.

Step 2 — Revocation checking

MechanismHow it behavesWatch for
CRLScheduled download from an HTTP or LDAP distribution point, cached locallyStale CRL if the download fails; the ignore expired CRL option masks real problems
OCSPPer-authentication query to an OCSP Services responder, with a local cacheResponder reachability from every PSN; the reject if OCSP returns UNKNOWN setting decides fail-open vs fail-closed

An OCSP responder unreachable from one PSN but fine from the others produces intermittent failures that follow the load-balancer hash — maddening to diagnose unless you check per-node.

Step 3 — Deriving the identity

The Certificate Authentication Profile (Administration → Identity Management → External Identity Sources) decides which X.509 field becomes the principal username:

  • Subject — Common Name
  • Subject Alternative Name (and which type: DNS, Email, Other Name / UPN)
  • Subject Serial Number
  • Subject, or SAN, as a whole

Machine certificates from Microsoft CA templates typically carry the FQDN in SAN-DNS; user certificates carry the UPN in SAN-Other Name. Choosing the wrong field produces a certificate that validates perfectly and then fails identity lookup — a completely different failure signature from a TLS problem, and important that your team can tell them apart.

Step 4 — Optional identity store lookup

  • Lookup only — confirm the object exists and retrieve group membership for authorisation conditions. The common case: you want Domain Computers or a specific group in your rule.
  • Binary comparison — ISE retrieves the certificate stored on the AD object and compares it byte-for-byte with the one presented. Much stronger, because a certificate issued by your CA to a decommissioned or non-domain object will fail. Requires the certificate to actually be published to AD, which many environments do not do.

Skip the lookup entirely and your authorisation policy can only match on certificate attributes, not AD groups. That constraint surprises people designing their first EAP-TLS policy.

Step 5 — Authentication result

ISE sends EAP-Success and moves to the authorisation policy. Note the ordering: identity resolution and group retrieval happen in authentication; the decision about VLAN, dACL and SGT happens afterwards, in authorisation, and can fail independently. A session showing a successful authentication but landing in a default-deny authorisation profile is a policy problem, not a certificate problem.

08Authorisation and CoA

Authentication answers who. Authorisation answers what they get, carried entirely in RADIUS attributes on the Access-Accept.

Policy evaluation order

  1. Policy set selection — first matching condition wins, usually on device type, location or NAS-Port-Type.
  2. Authentication policy — matches on protocol and attributes, selects an Allowed Protocols object and an identity source.
  3. Authorisation policy — evaluated top-down after a successful authentication. Conditions can use certificate attributes, AD groups retrieved in step 2, endpoint profile, posture status and time.
  4. Authorisation profile — the result, which becomes the attribute set below.

Attributes worth recognising in a capture

AttributePurpose
Tunnel-Type (64), Tunnel-Medium-Type (65), Tunnel-Private-Group-ID (81)Dynamic VLAN assignment. All three must be present — a missing one is a quiet failure
Cisco-AVPair = ip:inacl# or ACS:CiscoSecure-Defined-ACLDownloadable ACL. The named form makes the switch fetch the ACL in a separate RADIUS exchange, visible as an extra Access-Request
Filter-Id (11)References an ACL already on the switch. Simpler, but requires pre-provisioning
Cisco-AVPair = cts:security-group-tagTrustSec SGT assignment
url-redirect and url-redirect-aclPosture or guest redirection
Session-Timeout (27) + Termination-Action (29)Termination-Action = RADIUS-Request triggers re-authentication at timeout rather than tearing the session down. Getting this wrong causes periodic connectivity drops

A useful demo moment: show the same endpoint receiving two different authorisation profiles by changing one condition, with the capture showing exactly which attributes changed. It makes the authentication/authorisation split concrete.

Change of Authorisation RFC 5176

The session does not end at Access-Accept. ISE can reach back into an established session using CoA, which is how posture, profiling and quarantine work.

  • ISE is the CoA client, the NAD is the CoA server, on UDP 1700 for Cisco devices (3799 is the RFC default and is also seen).
  • CoA-Request (43) identifies the session by Audit-Session-Id or Calling-Station-Id; the NAD replies CoA-ACK (44) or CoA-NAK (45).
  • Types: Reauthenticate (last or rerun), Port Bounce, Session Terminate, Port Shutdown.

For EAP-TLS this matters in a specific way: reauthenticate with rerun re-evaluates policy without a new EAP handshake, while reauthenticate last forces the supplicant through the full handshake again. For a profiling-driven VLAN change you want the former — it is much faster, because there is no certificate exchange.

CoA failures are their own troubleshooting class. The NAD must have aaa server radius dynamic-author configured with a client statement for every PSN and the matching shared secret; a missing PSN there means profiling silently never takes effect.

09EAP-TLS with TLS 1.3

Worth covering, because the capture looks completely different and the first time someone sees it they assume something is broken.

Why it matters, and what it costs you

The privacy improvement is the headline. Under TLS 1.2, anyone with a span port on the access layer can inventory your endpoints by reading client certificates off the wire. Under TLS 1.3 they cannot. For a healthcare or regulated environment that is a meaningful control, not a nicety.

The operational cost is that your own troubleshooting gets harder. The workflow of look at the capture and read the certificate stops working, and you become dependent on ISE's own logging and on supplicant-side logs.

ISE support status

Track this carefully, because it has moved recently and differs by version:

  • From ISE 3.3, TLS 1.3 was introduced for the Admin GUI only, with that release's documentation explicitly noting that AAA communications and all internode communications do not support TLS 1.3.
  • ISE 3.4 is reported to extend TLS 1.3 to EAP-TLS and TEAP, and engineers testing with eapol_test against 3.4 patch 1 have found it negotiates.
  • Separately, TLS 1.2 remains the latest supported version when EAP-TLS is used as the inner method for EAP-FAST, TEAP and PEAP — inner-method EAP-TLS and standalone EAP-TLS are not the same case.
Before you state this as factVerify against the Compatibility Guide for your exact release and patch. Supplicant support is the other half of the equation and lags further — Windows, macOS, iOS and Cisco Secure Client each need checking, and in practice that is what gates deployment rather than ISE.

For a demo, eapol_test is the right instrument, because you can force the version explicitly rather than hoping a native supplicant negotiates it.

The diagnostic shortcut

Teach your team this one distinction and they will solve most cases themselves:

  • Failure before any certificate appears → cipher suite or TLS version mismatch.
  • Failure right after the ISE certificate → the supplicant does not trust ISE. Supplicant-side trust problem (12520, or 5440).
  • Failure right after the client certificate → ISE does not trust or cannot validate the client. Trust store, chain or revocation problem (12514).
  • Clean handshake, then failure → identity resolution, not cryptography (22056, 22049).
  • Clean authentication, wrong access → authorisation policy, not EAP at all.

Non-ISE symptoms worth knowing

  • Port shows Unauthorized with no RADIUS traffic — dot1x pae authenticator missing, or the global aaa configuration is absent.
  • Session falls through to MAB — the supplicant never answered the Identity request. Either no supplicant, or max-reauth-req exhausted.
  • Windows reports a disconnected network connection with a certificate-validation reason code — check Validate server certificate, the trusted root selection, and the Connect to these servers field in the profile.
  • Works for user, fails for machine (or vice versa) — the supplicant's authentication mode and which certificate store it reads. A machine certificate lives in the computer store and requires the supplicant to run as SYSTEM.
  • Intermittent, correlates with time of day — almost always certificate expiry around a renewal window, or an OCSP responder under load.

11Troubleshooting toolkit

On ISE

Live Logs → the detailed authentication report is the primary tool, and the Steps column is the part people underuse. It lists every EAP message in order with its ISE step code, so you can see exactly which message the conversation died on. Reading the step list top to bottom against the handshake table above is the whole technique.

  • Operations → Troubleshoot → Diagnostic Tools → TCP Dump — a capture on the PSN itself, filtered by endpoint MAC or NAD IP. The cleanest way to get the RADIUS leg.
  • Evaluate Configuration Validator — NAD config sanity check.
  • Debug Wizard → Debug Log Configuration — raise runtime-AAA and nsf-session on the relevant PSN, then collect prrt-server.log for deep EAP state machine detail.
  • The Certificate Authentication Profile and trust store pages, to confirm trust purposes and the selected X.509 field.

Event Viewer under Applications and Services Logs → Microsoft → Windows → Wired-AutoConfig (or WLAN-AutoConfig) gives reason codes. certutil -store -user My and certutil -store My confirm which store holds the certificate.
  • Cisco Secure Client NAM — DART bundle; the NAM log shows the EAP state machine directly.
  • wpa_supplicant / eapol_test — the reference supplicant and the best demo instrument. Prints every EAP and TLS message, lets you force a TLS version or cipher, exits with a clear pass/fail. If eapol_test succeeds with the same certificate a Windows machine fails with, the problem is unambiguously the Windows profile.

Capture strategy

Take captures at two points simultaneously and line them up by EAP Identifier:

The EAP Identifier byte is the same in both captures for the same logical message, which lets you prove whether a message was lost between the NAD and ISE or between the NAD and the endpoint. That correlation trick is worth demonstrating on its own.

Decryption in the labTo decrypt a TLS 1.2 EAP-TLS capture, force an RSA key-exchange cipher suite and load the ISE EAP certificate's private key into Wireshark. With ECDHE you cannot decrypt from the private key alone — which is the point of forward secrecy, and another good teaching moment.

12Demo runbook

A sequence that takes about forty minutes and ends with two deliberate break-fixes.

Minimum lab

  • One access switch with dot1x on a single port, pointed at one PSN.
  • One Windows endpoint with a machine certificate from your lab CA, and a second endpoint running eapol_test as the controllable reference supplicant.
  • Wireshark on a SPAN of the access port, plus ISE TCP Dump armed on the PSN.
  • Both captures started before you bounce the port.

Sequence to walk through

  1. Show the closed port first. show access-session interface ... with the port unauthorized. Point out that only EAPOL, CDP/LLDP and STP get through. This establishes the stakes.
  2. Bounce the port, then stop both captures. Everything that follows is reading the two captures.
  3. In the EAPOL capture, point at destination MAC 01:80:C2:00:00:03 and EtherType 0x888E. Then the Identity exchange, and note that the identity is in clear text.
  4. Switch to the RADIUS capture and find the matching Access-Request. Show EAP-Message holding the identical EAP bytes. This is the moment the pass-through concept lands — same bytes, different envelope.
  5. Show the EAP-TLS Start, flags 0x20, no payload.
  6. Expand the ISE certificate. Everyone can read it. Then expand CertificateRequest and show the CA DN list — and how long it is in your environment.
  7. Expand the client certificate. Point out the SAN field, and tie it to the Certificate Authentication Profile setting in ISE.
  8. Point at CertificateVerify and explain that this, not the certificate, is the proof. Pose the stolen-certificate question.
  9. Count the fragments and the acknowledgements. Show the 0x00 acks doing nothing but asking for more. Then count the round trips and multiply by your branch RTT.
  10. Show EAP-Success and the Access-Accept. Walk the authorisation attributes, and show MS-MPPE-Recv-Key / Send-Key present but encrypted.
  11. Open the ISE detailed report for the same session and read the Steps column alongside the capture. Same story, two views.
  12. show access-session ... details to show the end state: method EAP-TLS, authorisation profile, dACL, SGT, Audit-Session-Id.

Two deliberate break-fixes

These land harder than the happy path, because they prove the diagnostic method rather than just describing it.

Break 1 — remove the issuing CA's client-authentication trust purpose in ISE. Do not delete the CA, just untick Trust for client authentication and Syslog. Re-run. You get 12514, and in the capture the client certificate is sent and then the handshake dies. Walk the shortcut list: failure after the client certificate means ISE-side trust. Fix it live.

Break 2 — untrust the ISE EAP certificate's root on the supplicant. On the Windows endpoint, deselect the root CA in the network profile. Re-run. Now you get 5440 or 12520, and the handshake dies right after the ISE certificate — the mirror image. Same shortcut list, opposite side.

Run them in that order and the symmetry does the teaching for you.

Optional extensions, if time allows

  • Session resumption — bounce the port twice in quick succession and compare captures. The second has no certificates and far fewer round trips.
  • eapol_test with a forced TLS version — show a version-mismatch failure, then TLS 1.3 if your ISE release supports it for EAP. The opaque capture makes the privacy point instantly.
  • CoA — trigger a reauthentication from ISE Live Logs and show the CoA-Request on UDP 1700 and the resulting policy change without a new handshake.
  • A fragmentation experiment — lower the EAP fragment size on the Allowed Protocols object and watch the fragment count rise.

Standards referenced: RFC 5216 (EAP-TLS), RFC 9190 (EAP-TLS 1.3), RFC 3748 (EAP), RFC 3579 (RADIUS support for EAP), RFC 5176 (Change of Authorization), IEEE 802.1X and 802.1AE.

Cipher support, TLS version support and defaults move between ISE releases — confirm anything you intend to state as fact against the ISE compatibility guides and the cipher configuration guide for the exact version you are discussing.
