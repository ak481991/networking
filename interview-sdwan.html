01Architecture & planes

The four planes and what each one is forbidden from doing. Most candidates can name the components; the discriminating answers are that neither the controller nor the manager is ever in the data path, what the Validator actually knows, and why control-connection arithmetic is a design input rather than trivia.

Describe the Cisco SD-WAN architecture. What are the four planes?Core

Four separated planes. The orchestration plane (vBond, now SD-WAN Validator) authenticates devices and introduces them to the controllers. The management plane (vManage, now SD-WAN Manager) is configuration and monitoring. The control plane (vSmart, now SD-WAN Controller) distributes overlay routes and encryption keys and enforces centralized policy. The data plane is the WAN Edge routers, which are the only devices that ever carry user traffic.

Detail
PlaneComponentDoesIn the data path?
OrchestrationvBond / ValidatorAuthenticates edges and controllers, tells each edge where the controllers are, discovers whether the edge is behind NAT and what its mapped address isNo — and not in the control path either once bring-up completes
ManagementvManage / ManagerConfiguration, templates and config groups, monitoring, statistics, software upgrades, certificatesNo
ControlvSmart / ControllerOMP peer to every edge: redistributes overlay prefixes, TLOCs and service routes; distributes IPsec keys; applies centralized policyNo
DataWAN Edge (vEdge / cEdge)Builds IPsec or GRE tunnels to other edges, runs BFD across them, forwards traffic, enforces local policy and securityYes — only here

The connections between them are what the architecture actually is in operation: each edge builds a transient DTLS session to the Validator over every transport, then persistent DTLS or TLS sessions to the Controllers and to the Manager. OMP runs inside the Controller session; NETCONF runs inside the Manager session. Edge-to-edge tunnels are IPsec in the data plane and carry no control protocol at all beyond BFD.

Say the separation out loud, because it is the design's whole point"The controller distributes routes but never forwards a packet, and the manager configures everything but is not required for forwarding either — so if I lose all my controllers, existing data-plane tunnels keep carrying traffic." That sentence tells an interviewer you understand the failure model rather than the product diagram, and it sets up the obvious follow-up about what you lose while the controllers are down: no new tunnels, no route changes, no key rotation, and eventually the overlay degrades — but not instantly.
What they're testingWhether you know the control and management planes are out of the data path. The "traffic keeps flowing without controllers" consequence is the sign you have thought about it rather than memorised it.
What does vBond actually do, and why does it need a public IP address?Deep

Three jobs: authenticate the device, hand it the list of Controllers and Managers to connect to, and perform NAT discovery — telling the edge what its own post-NAT address and port look like from the outside. It needs a publicly reachable address because it is the one component every edge must reach before it has any other information, and because the NAT discovery only works if the edge's packets traverse the real NAT on the way to it.

Detail — the bring-up conversation
  1. The edge resolves or is configured with the Validator's address and opens a transient DTLS session to it, from every transport interface, on UDP 12346 by default.
  2. Mutual authentication happens using certificates — the edge validates the Validator's certificate chain, and the Validator checks the edge's certificate and that its chassis and serial number appear in the authorised WAN Edge list.
  3. The Validator observes the source address and port the packet arrived with and compares them with what the edge said about itself. That comparison is the NAT discovery: a difference means the edge is behind NAT, and the Validator reports the mapped address and port back.
  4. It hands back the Controller and Manager addresses.
  5. The edge builds persistent DTLS or TLS sessions to those, and the Validator's job is finished — the session to it tears down and the overlay runs without it.
The Validator cannot sit behind NAT in the normal designBecause its entire NAT-discovery function depends on seeing the edge's genuinely translated source address and port. If the Validator is itself behind a NAT that rewrites what it sees, or if it is reachable only on a private address, the discovery is wrong and edges behind NAT fail to build data-plane tunnels — control connections come up, BFD never does, and the symptom looks like a data-plane problem when the cause is in orchestration. In the supported design the Validator has a public address, or a static one-to-one translation configured so that what it sees is what the rest of the internet sees. Candidates who have only read the diagram say "it is just bootstrapping"; candidates who have deployed it know the NAT discovery is the part that bites.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What happens if the Validator is down?" — nothing, for sites already up: existing control and data connections are unaffected. What breaks is any new device onboarding, and the recovery of a site that loses all its control connections and has to start over. So it is not in the forwarding path, but it is in the recovery path, which is why it still needs redundancy.
  • "How do edges find it?" — configured address or DNS name in the system configuration, supplied by bootstrap file, PnP or DHCP-based zero-touch provisioning. A DNS name resolving to multiple addresses is the simple way to make it redundant.
What they're testingThe NAT discovery role above all — it is the Validator's least-known and most consequential job. "Not in the forwarding path but in the recovery path" is the framing that shows real understanding.
What is vSmart's role, and what exactly does it advertise?Deep

The Controller is an OMP route reflector for the overlay. Every edge peers with it and nothing else, so the control plane is a hub-and-spoke of OMP sessions even when the data plane is a full mesh. It redistributes three kinds of information between edges — OMP routes (prefixes), TLOC routes (tunnel endpoints) and service routes (where firewalls and other services live) — plus the IPsec keys edges need to talk to each other. And because every piece of reachability passes through it, it is also where centralized policy is enforced.

Detail — the three route types
TypeCarriesThink of it as
OMP routeA service-side prefix, with its originator, VPN, and the TLOC to reach it throughThe prefix advertisement — "10.20.5.0/24 lives at site 200, reach it via this TLOC"
TLOC routeA tunnel endpoint: system IP, colour, encapsulation, plus its public and private address and port, preference, weight and the encryption keyThe next-hop advertisement — "this is how you build a tunnel to that site"
Service routeA service advertised at a site — firewall, IDS, IDP, or a numbered custom serviceThe service-chaining advertisement

The split matters: a prefix is useless without the TLOC that reaches it. A policy that filters TLOCs changes which tunnels exist; a policy that filters OMP routes changes which prefixes are reachable over the tunnels that do exist. Confusing the two is the single most common cause of a centralized policy that does not do what its author intended.

The received-versus-advertised pair is the policy debugging toolCentralized policy is applied on the Controller, in a direction relative to the Controller — so a route can exist, be received, and still never be advertised onward. Comparing omp routes received with omp routes advertised on the edge, and the equivalents on the Controller, localises a policy problem in one step instead of reading the policy and hoping. Naming that habit is worth more in an interview than reciting the policy syntax.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "Do Controllers peer with each other?" — yes, Controllers establish OMP peering among themselves so they share a consistent view, and each also connects to the Manager and the Validator. The edges, though, peer only with Controllers; there is no edge-to-edge OMP.
  • "Why is the control plane hub-and-spoke when the data plane is a mesh?" — so that adding a site is O(1) in control-plane terms: one new OMP session rather than a new peering with every other site. It is the same argument as a BGP route reflector, and it is what makes a few thousand sites manageable.
What they're testingThe OMP route / TLOC route / service route distinction, which everything in policy depends on. The received-versus-advertised debugging move is the practitioner's answer.
What is vManage responsible for, and what does a cluster buy you?Deep

The Manager owns configuration, monitoring, statistics, software lifecycle and certificates — it is the single source of truth for device configuration, pushed over NETCONF. A cluster gives you redundancy and scale for that function, with the important caveat that it is a quorum-based cluster of databases, not a load balancer: the configuration database, statistics store, messaging and coordination services are distributed, so you deploy an odd number of nodes and you care about the cluster interface between them.

Detail
  • Configuration. Historically feature templates and device templates attached per device; the current model is config groups with feature profiles and variables, plus CLI add-on templates for anything the model does not express. Either way the device is managed, not hand-edited — local CLI changes get overwritten on the next push, and on a template-attached device they are rejected outright.
  • Monitoring and statistics. Device state, tunnel statistics, application visibility, alarms and events. This is where the statistics database lives and where most of the Manager's disk and compute go.
  • Software and certificates. Image repository, upgrades with activation and rollback, certificate signing and installation, the WAN Edge authorised list.
  • Policy authoring. Centralized policies are written in the Manager and activated onto the Controllers; localized policies are written there and pushed into device configuration. The Manager stores them; it does not enforce them.

Cluster shape: an odd number of nodes for quorum, with services spread across them — application server, configuration database, statistics database, messaging and coordination. A dedicated cluster interface carries the inter-node traffic, and the latency between nodes matters because the databases are synchronous enough to care.

Losing the Manager does not break the network, and people panic anywayWith the Manager down, forwarding continues, control connections stay up, and OMP keeps converging. What you lose is configuration change, new onboarding, statistics collection, and visibility — so you are flying the network blind but it is still flying. The real risks are different: a configuration database restored from a stale backup can push an old configuration across the estate, and a Manager whose disk fills up stops collecting statistics and can stop accepting configuration. Backup of the configuration database, and disk monitoring, are the two unglamorous operational items worth naming.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "Templates or config groups?" — config groups are the current direction and are easier to reason about at scale, with feature profiles and per-device variables. Many production estates still run feature and device templates. The honest answer names both, says which you have used, and notes that migration is a project rather than a toggle.
  • "Multitenancy?" — the Manager supports multi-tenant deployment where tenants share controllers but have logically separate configuration and visibility. Useful for a managed-service provider; it constrains some features and versions, so it is a deliberate architectural choice made at deployment time, not later.
What they're testingWhether you know the Manager is out of the forwarding path, and whether you have operated one — disk, database backup and template-versus-config-group are the markers.
vEdge or cEdge — what is the difference and does it matter?Deep

vEdge runs Viptela's own operating system; cEdge is IOS-XE in SD-WAN mode on ISR, ASR, CSR/C8000V and Catalyst 8000 platforms. It matters a great deal: cEdge inherits the IOS-XE feature set, which is where the on-box security, application optimisation and richer routing live, and it is the platform everything new is built for. The operational difference you notice first is that every show command gains an sdwan keyword and the configuration is IOS-XE rather than Viptela syntax.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "Can they coexist?" — yes, in the same fabric with the same controllers. What you cannot do is build a design that assumes a cEdge-only feature at a vEdge site, so the design is constrained by the lowest common denominator unless you segment it by site type deliberately.
  • "What is 'controller mode'?" — IOS-XE routers run either autonomous mode (traditional IOS-XE) or controller mode (SD-WAN, managed by the Manager). Switching between them is a reload and a reset of configuration, which makes it a migration event rather than a change.
What they're testingWhether you know cEdge is IOS-XE and where that buys you features. The "lowest common denominator constrains the design" point is the senior framing.
Why does the number of control connections matter, and what is controller affinity?Expert

Because by default every transport interface on every edge tries to build a control connection to every Controller and Manager — so connection count multiplies out as transports × controllers, and at scale that exhausts controller capacity and wastes WAN resources on sessions you do not need. Affinity and max-control-connections are the levers: you give controllers a group identifier, tell each edge which groups it prefers and how many controller connections it should hold, and the edge builds a deliberate subset instead of a full mesh of control sessions.

Detail
  • The arithmetic. Two transports and four Controllers is eight control connections per edge before you count the Manager. A thousand sites is eight thousand Controller sessions, and each one costs the Controller memory and OMP processing.
  • max-control-connections on the system caps how many Controller connections the edge holds in total.
  • Controller group ID is set on each Controller; affinity group preference on the edge lists the groups it prefers in order. The edge fills its allowance from its preferred groups and falls back to others if those are unavailable — so affinity is a preference with automatic failover, not a static pinning.
  • max-omp-sessions limits OMP sessions specifically, which is the resource that actually costs the Controller.
  • On a per-tunnel-interface basis you can also set max-control-connections 0 to stop a transport from building control sessions at all — the standard treatment for a transport you want for data only, such as an LTE link you do not want carrying control overhead.

Over-tuning this is how you cause a brown-outSetting max-omp-sessions or max-control-connections too low means an edge that loses its chosen Controllers may have no session left and stops receiving route updates — and because the data plane keeps forwarding on cached state, the symptom is delayed and confusing: traffic works, then gradually does not as things change. Set the allowance so that there is always at least one spare path to a controller in a different failure domain, and verify with show sdwan control affinity status rather than assuming the preference list did what you meant. The related trap is max-control-connections 0 on a transport that later becomes the only surviving transport, which leaves the site with a working data plane and no control plane.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "When would you actually use affinity?" — large estates and geographically distributed controller deployments, where you want European sites on European controllers for latency and blast-radius reasons, with cross-region fallback. Not in a small deployment, where it adds a failure mode for no benefit.
  • "What does the connections-history give you?" — the reason a connection failed, as local and remote error codes, which is the fastest route to the cause: certificate problems, the device not in the authorised list, a serial number mismatch, or the connection being blocked on the path. Reading that output rather than guessing is the whole skill in control-plane troubleshooting.
What they're testingWhether you treat control-connection count as a design parameter. Knowing connections-history exists, and the risk of over-restricting, both mark real operational exposure.

02Onboarding & bring-up

Getting a router from a box to a working overlay member. This section is heavily asked because it is where real deployments stall, and because the trust model — certificates, serial numbers and the organisation name — explains most bring-up failures.

How do you onboard a WAN Edge? Compare the methods.Core

Four methods, in decreasing order of how little anyone has to touch the device: automatic provisioning where the router finds the controllers over the internet, a bootstrap file you supply on removable media or flash, manual console configuration of the minimum needed to reach the Validator, and for virtual edges a one-time password because they have no hardware identity to prove.

Explain the certificate and trust model. What has to match?Deep

Everything authenticates mutually with certificates issued under one root of trust, and the organisation name is carried inside those certificates — so it has to be identical, character for character, on every controller and every edge. On top of that, an edge is only admitted if its chassis and serial number appear in the authorised WAN Edge list and are marked valid. Three independent checks: the certificate chain, the organisation name, and the authorised list.

Detail
  • Root CA options. The cloud-hosted default uses a commercial CA; you can also use Cisco PKI, or your own enterprise root CA. Whichever you choose, the same root chain has to be installed on every device, controllers included. Enterprise CA gives you control and gives you the job of renewing things on time.
  • Controller certificates. The Manager generates a certificate signing request for each controller; you get it signed and install the result. Until that is done the controllers will not talk to each other, let alone to edges.
  • Hardware edge identity. Physical routers carry a built-in identity certificate from manufacture — a trusted-platform board identity on vEdge hardware, a secure unique device identifier on IOS-XE platforms. The device proves it is that chassis without anyone installing anything.
  • Virtual edge identity. A virtual machine has no such anchor, so it uses a one-time password from the Manager plus a generated certificate. This is the step people miss when standing up a cloud edge.
  • The authorised list. Chassis and serial numbers come from the provisioning portal as a signed file, or sync automatically from the Smart Account. Each entry has a state — valid, invalid, or staging — and only valid gets a full control plane. Staging is the useful one: the device comes up and can be configured but is held out of the data plane until you promote it.

An organisation-name mismatch is invisible until you look for itA trailing space, a different case, a hyphen where someone typed an underscore — and the control connection fails with a mismatch error while every other setting looks correct. It is embedded in the certificates, so you cannot simply change it on one device to match another: changing it means re-issuing certificates. The second recurring trap is time: certificate validation is time-sensitive, so a device with a badly wrong clock and no working NTP fails validation for reasons that have nothing to do with certificates as such. Check show clock before you suspect PKI, because it costs five seconds and saves an afternoon.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What happens when a certificate expires?" — control connections drop and the device cannot re-establish them, so it eventually stops receiving route updates. The data plane survives for a while on existing state, which makes expiry a slow-moving outage rather than an instant one and therefore easy to miss until many sites are affected. Certificate expiry monitoring is a genuine operational requirement, not a nicety.
  • "What is the staging state for?" — pre-staging equipment: the device onboards, gets configured and verified, and is only moved to valid when it is ready to carry traffic. It is how you avoid shipping a router that joins the data plane the moment it is plugged in at the far end.
What they're testingThe three independent checks, and that the organisation name lives in the certificate. The clock point and the staging state are both signs of hands-on deployment work.
Walk me through the control connection bring-up, with ports and protocols.Deep

The edge builds a transient DTLS session to the Validator on UDP 12346, is authenticated and told where the controllers are, then builds persistent sessions to the Manager and the Controllers. The Manager session carries NETCONF and delivers configuration; the Controller session carries OMP, which delivers routes, TLOCs and keys. Only once OMP has the remote TLOCs do data-plane tunnels and BFD come up.

The sequence
  1. Transport up, default route present. Without a route out of the transport VRF nothing else happens, and this is the most common cause of a site that "will not onboard".
  2. DTLS to the Validator from each tunnel interface, UDP 12346. Mutual certificate authentication; organisation name checked; chassis and serial checked against the authorised list; NAT discovery performed.
  3. Validator returns the Manager and Controller addresses, and tells the edge its own mapped address and port if it is behind NAT.
  4. Persistent session to the Manager. The edge may briefly report that it has no configuration yet — a transient state, not a fault — until the template or config group is pushed.
  5. Persistent session to the Controllers, up to the configured allowance. OMP comes up inside it.
  6. OMP exchange — the edge advertises its own TLOCs and service-side routes, receives everyone else's, and receives the IPsec keys.
  7. Data plane. With remote TLOCs known, the edge builds IPsec tunnels and starts BFD across each tunnel. BFD up means the overlay is genuinely usable; control connections up alone does not.
ElementDetail
Base portUDP 12346. The Validator always listens there
Port hoppingOn failure the edge rotates through a small set of ports at fixed offsets from the base — 12346, 12366, 12386 and so on — so a single blocked or NAT-mangled port does not prevent bring-up. Enabled by default; disable it where a firewall policy must be deterministic

The distinction to make: control up is not the same as data up"Control connections up and BFD down" is an entirely different problem from "control connections down" — the first is almost always NAT, firewall or colour-related between the two edges, the second is certificates, the authorised list or reachability to the Validator. Separating those two before you start is the single most useful thing you can do, and saying so answers half the troubleshooting questions in this section before they are asked.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What ports do you ask the firewall team to open?" — UDP 12346 and the hopped range above it toward the Validator and Controllers, or TCP for TLS if you have chosen it, plus UDP 4500 and ESP for the data plane depending on encapsulation and whether NAT is in the path. And give them the range, because a policy permitting only 12346 breaks port hopping in a way that looks random.
  • "Why would you disable port hopping?" — to make the firewall rule deterministic and auditable. The trade-off is that you lose the automatic workaround when one port is mangled, so you only do it when you control the path end to end.
What they're testingThe ordering — Validator, then Manager and Controllers, then OMP, then BFD. Port hopping and port offset are the details most candidates do not have.
A new site's control connections will not come up. How do you work it?Deep

Read the failure reason rather than guessing. show sdwan control connections-history gives a local and remote error code for every attempt, and that code points straight at the cause — certificate, authorised list, organisation name, or simply no reachability. I would look there first, then at local properties, and only then at the configuration.

The order
  1. Is the transport up and is there a default route? Ping the Validator's address from the transport. If that fails, nothing else matters.
  2. show sdwan control connections-history — the error code for each attempt. This is the step that replaces guesswork.
  3. show sdwan control local-properties — organisation name, certificate state, serial number, system IP, site ID, and the discovered public address and port per transport, all in one output.
  4. On the Validator, confirm the device appears in the authorised list and is in a valid state.
  5. Clock and certificates — show clock, then the certificate chain.
  6. Path — is UDP 12346 and its hopped range permitted end to end, and is anything doing deep inspection or rewriting of that traffic?

You need to onboard a site with no DHCP, behind a provider CPE doing NAT, with only a single broadband circuit. How?Expert

Automatic provisioning is out because it needs DHCP and a resolvable path to the redirection service, so I would use a bootstrap file on flash or USB with the system configuration pre-loaded. Behind a provider NAT the edge will come up with a mapped public address discovered by the Validator; what then matters is the type of NAT and whether the provider CPE will allow the inbound data-plane traffic, so I would plan for the single-transport site to be a spoke and verify BFD rather than assume it.

What I would do, in order
  1. Bootstrap file generated from the Manager with the system IP, site ID, organisation name and Validator address, plus a statically addressed transport interface and a default route. Copied to flash before shipping, or to a USB stick for site staff to insert. No skilled hands needed at the site.
  2. Hold the device in staging in the authorised list so it onboards, gets its configuration and is verified, and only joins the data plane when I promote it. That turns a remote install into something I can check before it carries traffic.
  3. Expect NAT, and verify the discovery. show sdwan control local-properties shows the public address and port the Validator reported alongside the private ones, and the detected NAT type. That output is the single most useful thing on a NATed site.
  4. Plan for a restrictive NAT. If the provider CPE does symmetric NAT, two such sites cannot build a tunnel directly to each other — the mapped port differs per destination, so neither can predict the other. The design answer is that spoke-to-spoke between two symmetric-NAT sites does not work, so route that traffic via a hub, and make it explicit in the topology policy rather than discovering it as an outage.
  5. Permit the data plane. On the provider CPE, forward or permit the SD-WAN ports inbound, or place the edge in a DMZ with a one-to-one translation. Where the provider will not cooperate, a hub-only topology for that site is the honest design.
  6. Single transport means no transport redundancy. Say so. If the site matters, the answer is a second transport — cellular is the cheap one — and if it does not, accept the single point of failure deliberately rather than by accident.

03OMP

The Overlay Management Protocol is the heart of the fabric and the subject of the hardest questions in any SD-WAN interview. Know the three route types, the attributes, how best path is decided, how loops are prevented by site ID, and the advertisement limits that silently truncate your paths.

What is OMP, and how is it like and unlike BGP?Core

OMP is the control protocol of the overlay: it runs inside the secure session between each edge and the Controller and carries everything the fabric needs — prefixes, tunnel endpoints, services and encryption keys. It is like BGP in that it is a reliable, attribute-rich, policy-driven path-vector protocol reflected through a central point. It is unlike BGP in that it has no relationship to the underlay, it carries the keys as well as the routes, and it runs over exactly one session per controller rather than over arbitrary peerings.

Preference versus weight is the question behind the questionPreference chooses — the higher value wins and the other path is not used.   • "Where do you set these?" — TLOC preference and weight can be set locally on the tunnel interface, or centrally in a control policy on the Controller. Give me the OMP best path selection order.Deep

Validity first, then the two preference attributes, then where the route came from and with what metric, then deterministic tie-breaks on identifiers. In practice the first five steps decide essentially every real comparison, and the honest answer says so rather than reciting a tail that almost never gets reached.

The order
  1. Is the route valid? Its TLOC must be known and reachable. An OMP route whose TLOC has not resolved — because BFD is down or the TLOC was filtered — is not a candidate at all. This step causes more confusion than all the others combined, because the route is present in the table and still not used.
  2. Higher OMP route preference.
  3. Higher TLOC preference.
  4. Lower origin protocol, by a fixed order of preference: connected first, then static, then eBGP, then EIGRP internal, then OSPF intra-area, inter-area and external, then EIGRP external, then iBGP, then unknown.
  5. Lower origin metric — the metric the prefix had in the protocol it came from.
  6. Deterministic tie-breaks on the originator and TLOC identifiers, so the choice is stable and identical on every edge.

Beyond selection, equal-cost paths are installed up to the edge's ECMP limit, and weight applies on top of selection to share traffic unequally across TLOCs that survived it.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "How is this different from BGP best path?" — there is no AS path length step, because there is no AS path; site ID does the loop prevention instead.   • "Say you want a prefix preferred from one data centre." — raise the OMP route preference for that prefix from that site in a centralized control policy, or lower it from the other. What they're testingWhether you know validity depends on TLOC resolution, and whether you can give the order without padding it. Saying plainly that the tail rarely decides anything reads as confidence, not as a gap.
How does OMP prevent routing loops?Deep

Primarily by site ID: every OMP route carries the site it originated from, and an edge discards any route whose site ID matches its own. That single rule stops a prefix advertised out of a site from being re-learned back into it, which is the whole loop problem inside the fabric. The harder loops are at the boundary — where OMP meets OSPF, BGP or EIGRP on the service side — and there you prevent them with tags and filters, exactly as you would between any two routing protocols.

Detail
  • Site ID. Two routers at the same physical site normally share a site ID, which is what makes them behave as one site: they will not re-advertise each other's prefixes back into the site over the overlay, and they do not build tunnels to each other across the fabric. This is also why giving two routers at one site different site IDs is a design decision with consequences, not a cosmetic choice.
  • Originator. The system IP of the advertising edge, carried for identification and tie-breaking.
  • Overlay AS. An optional attribute for loop prevention where two overlays interconnect.
  • No edge-to-edge OMP. Because reflection is always through the Controller, there is no path for a route to circulate among edges.
The real loop risk is a dual-router site redistributing both waysTwo edges at one site, both running BGP to the LAN and both redistributing between BGP and OMP. A prefix learned from OMP by edge A is redistributed into BGP, crosses the LAN to edge B, and is redistributed by B back into OMP — where it now appears as a locally-originated prefix with a different site ID if the site IDs differ, so site-ID filtering does not catch it. You now have a prefix whose best path points back into your own site. The fix is the ordinary one for mutual redistribution: tag on the way out, filter on the way in, in both directions, and never rely on the fabric to protect you from a boundary you built yourself. Anyone who has run a dual-router site with mutual redistribution has met this, and saying it unprompted is a strong signal.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "Should two routers at a site share a site ID?" — normally yes, because that is what makes them one site for loop prevention, for tunnel formation and for policy matching. Give them different site IDs only when you deliberately want them to behave as separate sites — and then expect to handle the boundary loop risk yourself.
  • "What is OMP's administrative distance?" — high, so a prefix also learned locally by a service-side protocol wins over the overlay. That is usually what you want — local is better than across the WAN — but it is also how a leaked prefix on the LAN can quietly hijack traffic that should have crossed the fabric.
What they're testingSite ID as the mechanism, and whether you recognise the boundary as the real risk. The dual-router mutual-redistribution loop is the answer that distinguishes field experience.
What are send-path-limit and the ECMP limit, and how do they catch people out?Deep

Send-path-limit is how many equal paths the Controller advertises per prefix to each edge; the ECMP limit is how many the edge installs in its forwarding table. They catch people out because the path count multiplies faster than anyone expects — transports times remote sites times VPNs — and when the limit truncates, the paths that get dropped are not necessarily the ones you would have chosen to lose.

Detail
  • The multiplication. A prefix available at two data centres, each with two transports, is already four paths. Add a third transport or a third data centre and you are at six or nine. With a default limit of four, two of them never reach the edge — and the edge cannot tell you about paths it never received.
  • Where it bites hardest is a hub-redundant design with several transports, which is exactly the design people build for resilience. The symptom is that one data centre's path is simply absent from some branches, inconsistently, and the policy looks correct because the policy is correct.
  • The diagnosis is to compare the Controller's advertised paths with what the edge received. If the Controller shows more paths than the edge received, you have found your limit.
  • The ECMP limit on the edge is the second gate: paths can arrive and still not be installed. Both numbers need to exceed your real path count with headroom.

Raise both deliberately and then verify the count"I would set send-path-limit and the ECMP limit above my worst-case path count — transports times redundant hubs — and then verify by counting the paths the edge actually received, not by trusting the configuration." That is the answer. The reason it is a good interview question is that nothing fails, nothing alarms, and the only evidence is a path count that is lower than arithmetic says it should be. Also mention the cost: more paths means more OMP state and more memory on both the Controller and the edge, so the answer is "high enough with headroom", not "maximum".
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What is graceful restart in OMP for?" — so that losing the control session does not immediately invalidate the overlay routes. The edge keeps forwarding on the stale OMP routes for the duration of the graceful-restart timer, which is long by default — many hours — precisely so that a controller outage is not a data-plane outage. It is the mechanism behind "traffic keeps flowing without controllers".
  • "Would you shorten that timer?" — rarely. A short timer converts a controller outage into a forwarding outage, which is the opposite of what you want. The argument for shortening it is to avoid forwarding on genuinely stale information for a long time, and that is better handled by making the controllers redundant.
What they're testingWhether you know path counts multiply and that the limits truncate silently. Graceful restart as the reason the data plane survives is the connected insight.

A branch cannot reach one data centre subnet. The route is in the OMP table. Work it.Expert

The route being present tells me the problem is between "known" and "usable", so I would check in this order: is the path valid and installed or merely received; is its TLOC resolved and is BFD up on that tunnel; is a centralized policy filtering it or rewriting its TLOC; and is something in the service VRF — a more specific local route, or a missing reverse path — defeating it. Each step has one command and each eliminates a whole class of cause.

The sequence
  1. Is it chosen, installed and resolved? show sdwan omp routes vpn 10 <prefix> detail. If the path exists but is not installed, the detailed output's status flags and loss reason tell you which selection step eliminated it. If it is unresolved, the TLOC is the problem, not the route.
  2. Is the TLOC usable? show sdwan omp tlocs for that system IP and colour, then show sdwan bfd sessions. A TLOC advertised but with BFD down is the single most common cause of this symptom — the control plane knows about the far end and the data plane cannot reach it.
  3. Is policy involved? show sdwan policy from-vsmart shows exactly what the Controller has pushed to this edge. Compare omp routes received with what the Controller advertises. A control policy that filters the prefix, or sets a TLOC the edge cannot use, produces precisely this symptom.
  4. Is it a VPN membership problem? If the branch is not a member of the VPN at the Controller's view, it will not receive that VPN's routes at all — though then you would expect all of the VPN missing rather than one subnet, which is why the scope of the failure is diagnostic.
  5. Is the forwarding table what you think? show ip route vrf 10 <prefix>. A more specific local route — a static, a connected interface, a leaked prefix — beats the overlay path because of OMP's high administrative distance.
  6. Is the return path symmetric? The data centre must have a usable OMP route back to the branch. One-way reachability is frequently a one-way policy or a one-way advertisement problem, so check both directions before concluding anything.
  7. Is it data policy rather than routing? A centralized data policy with a drop action, or a localized ACL, blocks traffic while leaving routing perfect. show sdwan policy data-policy-filter and the access-list counters settle it.

04TLOCs & transport

Colours are the most misunderstood concept in Cisco SD-WAN, and the public-versus-private distinction decides whether two sites exchange private or post-NAT addresses. Get this section right and most tunnel-formation problems become obvious.

What is a TLOC?Core

A Transport Locator — the overlay's notion of a tunnel endpoint, identified by three things together: the edge's system IP, the colour of the transport, and the encapsulation. It is the next hop for every OMP route, and it carries everything another edge needs to build a tunnel to it: the private and public address and port, preference, weight, and the encryption key.

Detail
  • A router with two transports has two TLOCs — same system IP, different colours. A router running both IPsec and GRE on one transport has two TLOCs on that transport as well, because encapsulation is part of the identity.
  • The TLOC advertisement includes both the private address and port and the public (post-NAT) address and port. Which pair gets used depends on the colours involved, which is the next question and the thing that catches people out.
  • An OMP route is only usable if its TLOC is resolved — meaning a tunnel exists and BFD is up. A known TLOC with dead BFD makes every route pointing at it unusable while leaving them visible in the table.

Explain colours. What is the difference between a public and a private colour?Deep

A colour is a label for a transport, and it does two jobs: it is what policy matches on, and — critically — it decides which address pair two edges use to build a tunnel. When both TLOCs have private colours they use their private addresses. If either side has a public colour, both use their public, post-NAT addresses. That single rule explains most "the tunnel will not come up" tickets.

What is TLOC extension and when do you need it?Deep

It lets one edge router use another edge router's transport. The case is a dual-router site where each router is physically connected to only one circuit: without extension, router A has MPLS only and router B has internet only, so each has a single point of failure. TLOC extension cross-connects them over a direct link so each router can form tunnels over both transports, and the site survives losing either router or either circuit.

Detail
  • You configure tloc-extension on the interface facing the partner router, naming the transport interface being extended. Traffic from the partner's TLOC on that colour physically leaves through this router's transport port.
  • A direct inter-router link is required, and in practice you want a subinterface or a separate link per extended transport, so each extension has its own path.
  • For a private transport such as MPLS, the partner router needs an address on that circuit's subnet — which means the provider has to give you a second address, or you use a subinterface and the provider accepts both. This is the part that turns a design decision into a provider conversation, and it is why TLOC extension sometimes does not happen.
  • For a public transport, the extending router usually applies NAT so the partner's traffic can get out, since there is typically only one public address.

05Data plane — IPsec & BFD

The two things that make the data plane distinctive: encryption with no IKE negotiation between peers, and BFD that is always on and doubles as the measurement system for application-aware routing. Both are heavily asked and both have a gotcha that only shows up in production.

How is the data plane encrypted? There is no IKE — so where do the keys come from?Core

Each edge generates its own key locally and advertises it to the Controller inside its TLOC route. The Controller reflects it to the other edges, which then use it to encrypt traffic toward that TLOC. There is no peer-to-peer negotiation at all — which is the whole point: key distribution becomes a linear problem through the control plane instead of a full mesh of IKE sessions.

Detail
  • Why it matters at scale. Classic IPsec needs an IKE negotiation per tunnel, so a thousand-site full mesh is roughly half a million negotiations and the same number of security associations to maintain. Here each edge publishes one key per TLOC and the control plane does the distribution: linear, not quadratic. That single sentence is the answer to "why not just build DMVPN".
  • Encapsulation. ESP inside UDP, sourced from the TLOC's port, so it traverses NAT without needing anything special.
  • Cipher. AES with GCM by default, giving confidentiality and integrity in one pass.
  • Rekey. Each edge rotates its key on a timer — a day by default — and publishes the new one through OMP before the old one expires, so rotation is seamless and needs no coordination between peers.
  • Consequence worth naming: because keys ride on OMP, losing all control connections eventually means losing the ability to rekey. Graceful restart keeps you forwarding for a long time, but a prolonged controller outage is not indefinitely survivable.

Anti-replay drops caused by your own QoS are a real and confusing failureThe window tracks sequence numbers and discards anything too far behind the highest seen. Walk me through a packet from a branch LAN host to a data centre server across the overlay.Deep

The packet arrives in a service VRF, gets a route whose next hop is a TLOC rather than an IP address, may be re-steered by data policy or application-aware routing, is encapsulated with a VPN label so the far end knows which VRF to land it in, wrapped in ESP and UDP, and routed out of the transport in the global table. The far edge reverses it. The label is the part people leave out and it is the part that makes segmentation work.

The walk
  1. Ingress, service side. The packet lands on a LAN interface bound to VRF 10. Any inbound localized policy — an ACL, a policer, a marking — applies here.
  2. Route lookup in VRF 10. The match is an OMP route whose next hop is a TLOC: system IP, colour, encapsulation. Note what did not happen — no lookup in the global table for the destination, because the destination is reachable only through the overlay.
  3. Policy may override the route. A centralized data policy can set a different local TLOC, or a next hop, or drop the packet. Application-aware routing can move it to a different colour based on the SLA class its application matches. Policy outranks the routing decision, which is why "the route says MPLS but the traffic is on the internet" is usually policy and not a routing bug.
  4. Encapsulation. The original IP packet is prefixed with the VPN label identifying VRF 10 at the far end, then protected by ESP, then wrapped in UDP sourced from the local TLOC's port and destined to the remote TLOC's port, then an outer IP header with the local transport address as source and the remote transport address as destination — public or private according to the colours involved.
  5. Global-table routing. The encapsulated packet is now ordinary transport traffic and is routed out of the transport interface using the global routing table and its default route. QoS on the physical interface applies here, to the encapsulated packet — which is why the outer DSCP matters and why per-tunnel QoS exists.
  6. Across the underlay. The provider sees a UDP flow between two transport addresses and knows nothing about VRFs or the inner addressing.
  7. Remote edge. Decapsulate, verify integrity, decrypt, read the VPN label to select VRF 10, then route in that VRF to the LAN. Service-side egress policy applies.
  8. Return traffic repeats it in reverse — and may take a different path, which matters for anything stateful in the middle.
Say the overhead and the MTU consequenceOuter IP, UDP, ESP and the label add up to something in the region of sixty to eighty bytes depending on encapsulation and options, so a 1500-byte LAN MTU does not fit in a 1500-byte transport. Either the transport supports a larger MTU, or you clamp TCP MSS and clear the don't-fragment bit, or large flows mysteriously fail while pings succeed. Volunteering that connects the packet walk to the most common real-world overlay problem, and it is the natural bridge to the QoS and troubleshooting sections.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "Where is the VRF decided on the far end?" — entirely by the label. There is no separate tunnel per VPN; one set of tunnels carries every segment, demultiplexed by label. That is why segmentation costs almost nothing in tunnel count and why the label is in the TLOC and route advertisements.
  • "Which DSCP does the provider see?" — the outer header's, which the edge sets — by default copying or deriving it from the inner marking. If the provider's MPLS QoS is supposed to honour your classes, the outer marking is the only thing it can act on, so verifying what is actually in the outer header is a real task rather than a theoretical one.
What they're testingWhether you include the VPN label and know policy can override the route. The MTU arithmetic shows you have debugged this in production.
What does the fabric do when BFD goes down on one tunnel?Deep

That TLOC becomes unresolved locally, so every OMP route using it is no longer a valid path and traffic moves to the remaining paths — within the detection time, which is about seven seconds on defaults. The edge also withdraws nothing from OMP, because the route is still perfectly valid from everyone else's point of view: this is a local decision based on local measurement, and that distinction matters.

Detail
  1. Detection after the multiplier's worth of missed hellos — seven seconds by default, tunable per colour.
  2. The TLOC becomes unresolved on this edge, so OMP routes pointing at it drop out of the forwarding table.
  3. Traffic re-converges onto the other paths. If another TLOC pair exists — a second transport, or the same transport to a redundant hub — the move is fast and the user may not notice. If no alternative exists, the destination is simply unreachable.
  4. Application-aware routing works differently: it does not need BFD to go down. A tunnel that is up but failing its SLA on loss, latency or jitter will have the affected application classes moved off it while everything else continues to use it. So one circuit can be carrying bulk traffic and not voice.
  5. On recovery, BFD comes back, the TLOC resolves, routes become valid again, and traffic returns. There is no hold-down by default, which is why a flapping circuit produces flapping traffic.

BFD is flapping on the internet transport at twenty branches, MPLS is fine. Work it.Expert

  2. Do the twenty sites share a provider or a region? If so, it is almost certainly the underlay and your next conversation is with the carrier, armed with timestamps.
  3. When did it start, and what changed? A central policy activation, a template push, a software upgrade, an MTU change, or a new QoS policy. Twenty sites changing behaviour simultaneously is usually something you did.

  2. Congestion or policing on the underlay. Check that BFD's high-priority marking is actually honoured by the shaper, and look at tunnel and interface drop counters. A newly deployed QoS policy that queues BFD with the bulk classes is a classic self-inflicted version of this.
  3. NAT timeout. Check the NAT refresh behaviour and the provider CPE's UDP timeout. This one has a distinctive signature: it happens at night.

06Centralized policy

Where the fabric's behaviour is actually decided. Two facts carry more interview weight than anything else here: direction is relative to the Controller, and the default action is to reject or drop — so a policy that matches nothing blackholes everything.

What are the types of policy in Cisco SD-WAN?Core

Two families. Centralized policy is authored in the Manager and activated onto the Controllers — it shapes the overlay by changing what the Controllers advertise, or by being pushed down to edges as a data policy they enforce. How do you build a hub-and-spoke topology with control policy?Deep

By filtering what the Controller tells the spokes. Spokes default to a full mesh because they learn every other site's TLOCs and routes; a control policy applied outbound to the spoke site list accepts only the hub TLOCs and rewrites spoke routes to point at the hub. The spokes then have no way to build spoke-to-spoke tunnels, because they never learn the TLOCs they would need.

Control policy's default action is rejectOmit default-action accept and everything your sequences did not explicitly accept is discarded — which in a control policy means routes and TLOCs vanish and sites go dark. Follow-ups to expect
  • "Data policy or application-aware routing for steering?" — data policy sets a path statically; application-aware routing chooses a path dynamically against measured SLA. Use data policy when the requirement is "this traffic always goes there" — breakout, a specific destination, a service chain. Use application-aware routing when the requirement is "this traffic needs a path that meets these conditions". Mixing them up is the commonest design error in this area.
  • "What happens if both apply?" — a data policy that sets a local TLOC constrains what application-aware routing can choose from, so they interact and the result can surprise you. Keep their scopes separate where you can, and when they must overlap, work out and document which one is deciding.

A centralized policy is not doing what you expected. How do you verify it?Deep

Find out what each device actually received, rather than re-reading what you wrote. show sdwan policy from-vsmart on the edge is the authoritative answer to "what policy is in force here", and on the Controller side you compare what it received with what it advertised. Nine times out of ten the policy is correct and the direction, the site list or the apply block is wrong — so the policy is in force somewhere it was not meant to be, or nowhere at all.

The checklist
  1. Is it activated at all? A policy saved in the Manager and not activated onto the Controllers does nothing. Check the Controller's running configuration, not the Manager's policy list.
  2. Did this edge receive it? show sdwan policy from-vsmart. If the device is not in the site list, nothing arrives — and this is the most common cause by a wide margin.
  3. Is the site list right? Site ID ranges that do not include the site you are testing, or a list that was correct before a new site block was allocated. Check the actual site ID on the device against the list.
  4. Is the direction right? For control policy, in and out are relative to the Controller. For data policy, from-service and from-tunnel are relative to the edge. Two different conventions in one feature set, and mixing them up produces a policy that does something, just not what you wanted.
  5. Are the sequences matching? count on each sequence plus show sdwan policy access-list-counters. Zero hits means the match is wrong, not the action.
  6. What is the default action? Reject for control policy, drop for data policy. If a lot of things broke at once, this is why.
  7. For routing effects, compare omp routes received with omp routes advertised at the Controller and the edge, and look at the per-path detail for the attribute the policy was supposed to set.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "So how do you actually enforce VPN 20 through the firewall?" — at the route level: spoke prefixes in VPN 20 are advertised with the hub TLOCs, so spokes send that VPN's traffic to the hub even though a direct tunnel exists. 07Application-aware routing

The feature SD-WAN gets sold on, and the one most often deployed badly. The content that matters: where the measurements come from, what happens when no path meets the SLA, the difference between strict and best-effort fallback, and the first-packet problem in application recognition.

What is application-aware routing, and how is it different from a data policy that steers traffic?Core

Application-aware routing picks a path dynamically, by measured performance: you define an SLA in terms of loss, latency and jitter, bind an application to it, and the edge continuously chooses a tunnel that currently meets it. A data policy steers statically — this traffic goes on that TLOC, regardless of how the circuit is behaving. One expresses intent about quality, the other expresses intent about path.

So application-aware routing is only as responsive as those timers, which is why the poll interval is the single most important setting in this feature.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "How many SLA classes can you have?" — a small number, four in most releases, which is a real design constraint: you cannot give forty applications their own SLA.   • "Where do the thresholds come from?" — from what the application needs, not from what the circuit delivers. Users report choppy voice. Application-aware routing says the SLA is being met. What is going on?Expert

The most likely answer is that the measurement is right and too slow: with the default poll interval and multiplier, the SLA average covers roughly an hour, so a circuit that is bad for two minutes at a time looks fine. After that I would check whether the traffic is even matching the policy, whether QoS is the actual problem, whether the path is asymmetric, and whether what is degrading is something BFD does not measure.

The causes, in the order I would check them
  1. Averaging window too long. Poll interval times multiplier is the averaging period, and on defaults that is an hour. Short bursts of loss or jitter never move it. Check the configured timers and compare the SLA view with the raw per-tunnel statistics over a short window. This is the most common answer by a wide margin.
  2. The traffic is not matching the policy. count on the voice sequence, then the counters. If the sequence has no hits, classification is failing — the flow is being recognised as something generic, or the first-packet problem means short call-setup exchanges never get classified. The SLA class is irrelevant to traffic that never matched it.
  3. QoS, not path selection. If voice is queued behind bulk traffic on the egress interface, the circuit's loss and latency can be fine end to end while the voice packets specifically are delayed and jittered inside your own queues. Check the policy map, the queue drops, and whether the voice marking survives from the LAN through to the outer header.
  4. Asymmetry. Application-aware routing decides per direction, at each end, from that end's measurements. The branch may send on MPLS while the data centre replies over the internet. For voice, one bad direction is enough to ruin the call, and a one-ended investigation will not find it — look at both ends.
  5. Something BFD does not measure. BFD samples the tunnel with its own small, high-priority packets. It does not measure what happens to a 200-byte voice packet in a congested queue behind your QoS policy, it does not see the LAN, and it does not see the far-side handset or the SBC. A clean tunnel with a broken call is entirely possible, and the next step is to measure the actual call path rather than the tunnel.
  6. MTU and fragmentation affecting signalling or media, which produces distinctive partial failures rather than general choppiness — worth a check if the pattern is odd.

Localized policy is ordinary device configuration delivered by the Manager. The interview value is almost all in QoS: the eight-queue model with queue 0 reserved, the rewrite rule that sets the outer marking, and per-tunnel QoS — which solves a problem classic WAN QoS cannot address at all.

Describe the QoS model on a WAN Edge. How many queues, and what is queue 0?Deep

Eight queues. Queue 0 is the low-latency queue and it is reserved for control traffic — BFD, OMP and the control sessions — which is exactly right, because losing BFD to congestion would tear down tunnels that are merely busy. Your traffic classes go into queues 1 to 7, mapped from forwarding classes, with bandwidth and buffer allocations and a drop policy per queue, all shaped to the circuit's real rate.

Detail — the chain
  1. Classify. A class map matches traffic and assigns a forwarding class — either in localized policy via an access list, or in centralized data policy with set forwarding-class.
  2. Map class to queue. Each forwarding class is bound to a queue number.
  3. Schedule. Per queue: a bandwidth share, a buffer share, a scheduling discipline, and a drop policy — tail drop or random early detection, which you want on TCP-heavy classes.
  4. Shape. A shaping rate on the interface matching the circuit's actual deliverable rate, so queueing happens in your router where you control it rather than in the provider's where you do not.
  5. Rewrite. A rewrite rule sets the outer header's marking on the way out.

Shape below the circuit rate, and never starve queue 0Two mistakes. First, shaping at the line rate rather than the deliverable rate: on a 10 Mbps service delivered over a gigabit handoff, the interface will happily send at a gigabit and the provider will police it down, so your careful queueing happens nowhere and loss happens in the provider's network, invisible to you and affecting whichever packets it chooses. Shape at or just under the committed rate, and remember the shaper sees the encapsulated packet, so the overhead counts against it. Second, a QoS policy that gives the priority class a large share and leaves control traffic to contend with the rest: BFD must stay in the low-latency path, and its high DSCP marking has to be honoured end to end, or you get tunnel flaps under load that look like circuit faults.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "How many classes should a branch have?" — four or five: real-time, interactive or business-critical, bulk, scavenger, and default. More than that and nobody can reason about it, the provider will not match it anyway, and the marginal benefit is nil. Saying this is better than listing eight.
  • "Where does the forwarding class come from?" — either localized classification at the interface, or centralized data policy with set forwarding-class, which is useful because the same policy that recognises the application can also assign its queue. Mixing both sources in one design is how you end up with traffic in the wrong queue and nobody able to say why.
What they're testingEight queues with queue 0 reserved for control. Shaping below the deliverable rate, and the overhead counting against the shaper, are the practical details.
What problem does per-tunnel QoS solve, and how does it work?Deep

A hub with a large circuit can overwhelm a branch's small one. The hub's egress QoS shapes to the hub's rate, so if it sends 50 Mbps toward a branch with a 10 Mbps downlink, the excess is dropped somewhere in the provider network — with no regard for your classes and nothing the branch can do about it. Per-tunnel QoS has each spoke advertise its downstream bandwidth, and the hub builds a separate shaper per tunnel sized to each branch, so the queueing and the class-aware dropping happen at the hub where you control them.

Detail
  1. The spoke advertises its downstream bandwidth and declares itself a spoke for tunnel QoS purposes.
  2. The hub is configured in hub mode and, for each tunnel, instantiates a child shaper at the spoke's advertised rate with the full class policy underneath it.
  3. The result is a hierarchy: an aggregate shaper for the hub's own circuit, then a per-spoke shaper, then the per-class queues. Congestion toward a slow branch is handled at the hub, so voice toward that branch is protected and bulk is the thing that gets dropped.
  4. Adaptive QoS goes further for broadband, where the deliverable rate varies: it measures the achievable bandwidth and adjusts the shaper between a configured minimum and maximum, rather than relying on a static number that is wrong most of the time.

How does DSCP marking work across the overlay? What does the provider see?Deep

The inner packet keeps its original marking inside the encryption; the provider can only see the outer header, which the edge sets. By default the outer marking derives from the inner one, and a rewrite rule lets you set it explicitly per forwarding class and loss priority. If your MPLS provider's QoS is supposed to honour your classes, the outer marking is the entire contract — and verifying what is actually in it is a real task, not a formality.

Detail
  • Trust at ingress. Decide whether you believe markings arriving from the LAN. On an access LAN the answer is usually no — re-mark at the edge based on your own classification. From a trusted upstream, such as a voice platform or a campus that already enforces a QoS model, trust and preserve.
  • Internal handling. Classification assigns a forwarding class, which drives the queue.
  • Rewrite on egress. The rewrite rule maps forwarding class plus loss priority to an outer DSCP or CoS value. This is what the provider acts on.
  • At the far end, decapsulation restores the inner packet with its original marking intact, so the receiving LAN sees what the sender set — the overlay has not disturbed it.

Segmentation in Cisco SD-WAN is nearly free — one set of tunnels carries every segment, demultiplexed by label — which is why people create too many VPNs and then need route leaking to undo it. Service insertion is the related topic, and its failure behaviour is the part worth knowing.

Explain service insertion and service chaining. What happens when the service goes down?Deep

A site advertises that it hosts a service — a firewall, an IDS, or a numbered custom service — as a service route in OMP. A control policy then sets that service on the traffic you want inspected, so the fabric routes it via the site hosting the service before it reaches its destination. Chaining is just doing this more than once. The important part is the failure behaviour: if the service is unreachable and you have not enabled tracking, traffic can be black-holed toward a device that is not there.

Detail
  1. At the service site, the edge is configured with the service type, its VRF and the appliance's address. The edge advertises a service route and the Controller reflects it.
  2. In control policy, set service on matching routes makes other sites send that traffic via the service site.
  3. The service site receives the traffic, hands it to the appliance in the service VRF, gets it back, and forwards it onward.
  4. Chaining composes these — firewall at one site, then an inspection service at another — which is powerful and makes the path considerably harder to reason about and to troubleshoot.

The segmentation is the easy part; the discipline is in making sure nothing later connects the two.

The design
  1. A dedicated service VPN — say VPN 30 — carrying only the guest SSID's subnet. One VLAN, one VRF, one purpose.
  2. No leaking to corporate VPNs. Ever. Not for DNS, not for DHCP, not for the captive portal. Guest DNS resolves through a public or filtering resolver; guest DHCP is served locally by the edge. Every "small exception" for a shared service is a hole in the only boundary this design has.
  3. VPN membership policy so that guest routes are not distributed around the fabric at all. A branch has no reason to learn another branch's guest subnet, and the control plane is a boundary worth using as well as the data plane.
  4. Local breakout with NAT. A default route in VPN 30 leaked from the global table, transport-side NAT to the internet circuit, and a data policy that keeps that traffic local. Guest traffic should never consume WAN capacity or appear at a data centre.
  5. No NAT fallback for guest. If the branch's internet dies, guests lose internet — which is correct. Falling back over the overlay would send guest traffic through the corporate data centre, which is exactly what this design exists to prevent.
  6. Treat the branch as an internet edge. On-box zone-based firewall permitting guest outbound only, no inbound, and no reachability to the router's own management or to the transport addressing. DNS-layer filtering or a secure gateway for acceptable use and malware. URL filtering if required by policy.
  7. Rate-limit it. A policer on the guest VPN, and the lowest QoS class, so guest traffic cannot degrade the business traffic sharing the circuit.
  8. Protect the router itself. allow-service tightened on the internet-facing TLOC so management services are not exposed, and no guest-side access to the device.

10DIA, SIG & Cloud OnRamp

How branch traffic reaches the internet and the cloud, which is where most of SD-WAN's business case actually lives. The questions worth preparing: the three breakout options and their trade-offs, why a SIG tunnel needs a layer 7 tracker, and how Cloud OnRamp for SaaS decides a path.

What are the options for internet access from a branch, and what do they cost you?Core

Three. Backhaul everything to a data centre and out through the existing security stack. Direct internet access at the branch with local security. Or a secure internet gateway — break out locally but tunnel the traffic to a cloud security service. Most real designs end up using all three for different traffic, and the ability to say which traffic goes which way is the answer.

Detail

How do SIG tunnels work, and why does the tracker matter so much?Deep

The Manager holds credentials for the security provider and calls their API to provision tunnels automatically — you declare that an edge should have SIG tunnels and the tunnels, and the provider-side configuration, are created for you. Traffic is sent into them by a route or a data policy action. The tracker matters because a tunnel can be perfectly up while the service behind it is broken, and without a layer 7 health check the edge keeps feeding traffic into a hole.

Detail
  1. Credentials and automation. Provider API credentials are configured once in the Manager. When you attach a SIG configuration to an edge, the Manager creates the tunnels on both sides — which is the real value, because doing this by hand for two hundred branches with two tunnels each is four hundred manual tunnel builds.
  2. Two tunnels, two points of presence. Normally a primary and a secondary to different provider locations, active/backup or load-shared. One tunnel is a single point of failure for all your internet traffic.
  3. Encapsulation. IPsec or GRE, depending on the provider and the design.
  4. Getting traffic in. Either a route in the service VPN pointing at the SIG service, or a data policy action that sends matching traffic to it. The route is simpler; the data policy lets you be selective, which is what you want when some traffic goes direct and some goes to the gateway.
  5. The tracker. An endpoint tracker probes a URL through the tunnel at an interval. If the probe fails, the edge marks the tunnel down and uses the secondary, or falls back to direct access or the overlay according to your configuration.

Follow-ups to expect
  • "What should the fallback be?" — the secondary tunnel first. Beyond that it is a policy decision: fall back to direct internet access, which keeps users working but bypasses the security stack, or fail closed, which keeps the policy intact and takes internet access down. Most organisations choose direct access with on-box filtering as a degraded mode, and the point is to choose it deliberately and tell the security team what the degraded mode looks like.
  • "What are the scale limits?" — tunnels have throughput and session ceilings per edge and per provider tunnel, so a large branch may need more than the default pair, and a very large site may be better served by a dedicated connection to the provider. Asking what the per-tunnel limits are before designing for two hundred branches is the kind of question that saves a redesign.
What they're testingThat the Manager automates provisioning through the provider's API, and that a layer 7 tracker is mandatory rather than optional. The simultaneous-failure-everywhere framing is what makes the point land.
How does Cloud OnRamp for SaaS choose a path?Deep

It actively probes the application's own endpoints over each candidate path and scores them. Candidate paths are the branch's own direct internet access and any gateway sites — sites you designate to provide internet egress for others. From the probe results it computes a quality score per application per path, and steers that application's traffic to the best-scoring path. So unlike application-aware routing, which measures your tunnels, this measures the path to the actual service.

Detail
ElementWhat it is
ProbingHTTP probes to the application's published endpoints, sent over each candidate path
Quality scoreA score derived from loss and latency, per application per path — the number the decision is made on
Direct internet access pathThe branch's own breakout, if it has one
Gateway siteA designated site — data centre or regional hub — whose internet egress other sites can use for SaaS
Per-application decisionDifferent applications can take different paths from the same branch, because they are hosted in different places

Why it is different from application-aware routing: application-aware routing measures loss, latency and jitter on your overlay tunnels using BFD. Cloud OnRamp measures the path to a third party's service, which your tunnels do not traverse. Both are performance-based steering, and they answer different questions — "which of my tunnels is healthy" versus "is this cloud app better reached locally or through the data centre".

Follow-ups to expect
  • "What if the branch has no direct internet access?" — then the candidates are the gateway sites only, and the feature still earns its place by picking the best gateway — which for a geographically spread estate with two or three regional egress points is a real decision rather than a trivial one.
  • "Does the probing cost anything?" — a small amount of traffic and some state per application per path, so you enable it for the applications that matter rather than everything in the catalogue. And because it depends on reaching the application's published endpoints, a security policy or a filtering resolver that blocks the probes breaks the scoring while leaving the applications working, which is a confusing failure worth knowing about.
What they're testingThat it probes the real service rather than your tunnels, and that gateway sites are a concept. The Microsoft 365 categorisation point is a strong practical marker.
What does Cloud OnRamp for Multicloud do?Deep

It extends the fabric into the public cloud by deploying virtual edges there and wiring them to the cloud provider's own transit construct, then automating the tedious part — discovering the workload networks, mapping them to your service VPNs, and keeping the routing consistent. The result is that a cloud workload network is reachable as a site in the fabric with the same segmentation and policy as a branch.

Detail
  • Virtual edges in the cloud. A pair of virtual routers in a transit network, joining the fabric as a site with its own TLOCs over the cloud provider's internet or private connectivity.
  • Integration with the cloud's transit. Rather than peering with every workload network individually, the edges attach to the provider's transit construct, and the workload networks attach to that. One attachment point, provider-native routing beneath it.
  • Discovery and mapping. The Manager can enumerate the workload networks in the cloud account, and you map them — often by tag — to service VPNs. That mapping is the automation: it turns "add this new application network to the WAN" into a tagging exercise rather than a routing project.
  • Private connectivity. Where the internet is not acceptable, an interconnect provider gives you private circuits into the cloud, with the same fabric constructs on top.
  • Colocation. A related model places SD-WAN plus a chain of network services in a colocation facility, so a region gets a shared egress and inspection point without building a data centre.
The honest framing"The value is consistency — the cloud network becomes a site with the same segmentation and the same policy as a branch, so I am not maintaining a separate cloud networking model with its own route tables and its own security boundaries." Then the caveat worth volunteering: you are now running routers you have to size, patch, license and monitor inside someone else's infrastructure, with its own failure modes, its own bandwidth characteristics and its own bill. For an estate already running SD-WAN everywhere that is usually worth it; for an organisation whose cloud footprint is a handful of SaaS applications it is solving a problem they do not have, and the cloud provider's own networking is simpler. Knowing when not to use it is a better answer than describing the feature.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "How is it different from just using the cloud provider's own transit?" — the provider's transit connects cloud networks to each other and to a VPN or a direct circuit, but it does not understand your segments, your application policy or your SLA classes. The fabric does, and the integration is about extending those rather than replacing the provider's plumbing — it sits on top of it.
  • "Where does internet egress happen for cloud workloads?" — a design decision with the same shape as the branch question: the cloud's own egress, your fabric's egress, or a secure gateway. Recognising that it is the same trade-off as the branch breakout question, with the same answers, is a nice piece of joined-up thinking.
What they're testingWhether you understand it extends the fabric rather than replacing cloud networking, and whether you can say when it is not worth it. The operational cost caveat is the mature answer.
Two hundred branches, each with MPLS and broadband. Design internet access. Defend your choices.Expert

A three-way split: corporate applications over the overlay, a short and owned list of verified SaaS breaking out directly for performance, and all other internet traffic out locally through a secure internet gateway so there is exactly one security policy across two hundred sites. Backhaul retained only for traffic that genuinely must originate from data centre address space. The reasoning is that performance wants local egress and security operations wants centralised policy, and SIG is the only option that gives both.

Defending it
  1. Why not all DIA? Because it creates two hundred internet edges, each needing a firewall policy, URL filtering, patching, log collection and someone to answer for it. The security team cannot operate that, and inconsistency between sites is the real risk, not the technology.
  2. Why not all backhaul? Because it wastes the broadband circuits you are paying for, puts cloud traffic through two WAN legs, and makes the data centre's internet capacity the constraint on every branch. It also makes the data centre a single point of failure for internet access across the estate.
  3. Why not all SIG? Because the gateway hop costs latency for applications that are right next door, and because per-edge tunnel limits and the subscription cost both scale with volume. Breaking out a small verified list directly takes the highest-volume, most latency-sensitive traffic out of the gateway and makes the economics work.
  4. Why an owned SaaS list? Because the direct path bypasses the security stack entirely, so the list is a security control. It needs a named owner, a review cadence and a change process — not a configuration someone added once. This is the part of the design that decays.
What I would verify before going live
  • Two SIG tunnels per branch to different points of presence, with a layer 7 tracker and a defined, documented degraded mode.
  • What happens when broadband fails: does internet traffic fall back over MPLS to a data centre breakout, and is the data centre sized for two hundred branches doing that at once? Usually not — so the honest answer may be partial fallback or accepting degradation, decided in advance.
  • The branch firewall posture even with SIG: the router is still internet-facing, so allow-service tightened, no management exposure, inbound denied.
  • Capacity at the gateway and at the data centre under a correlated failure, which is the scenario nobody sizes for.
  • Logging: that branch internet activity reaches the same place as everything else, because a security team that cannot see two hundred branches' traffic has lost visibility compared with the backhaul design they gave up.
Interviewers are listening for the correlated-failure questionAnyone can list the three options. The distinguishing answer asks what happens when every branch fails over at once — to the data centre, or to the secondary gateway point of presence — and whether that path is sized for it. Two hundred branches falling back simultaneously is a capacity event, not a routing event, and designs that work perfectly in single-failure testing fall over in a regional outage. Raising it unprompted, and saying you would rather accept a documented degradation than pretend the fallback is free, is what a senior design answer sounds like.
What they're testingWhether you split by traffic type and can defend each choice against the alternatives. The owned-list-as-a-security-control and correlated-failure-capacity points are the two that mark real design experience.

OMP has a high administrative distance. Why does that matter?Deep

Because it means a prefix learned locally — by BGP, OSPF, EIGRP or a static route in the service VRF — beats the same prefix learned from the fabric. Usually that is exactly right: a local path is better than crossing the WAN. But it is also the mechanism behind two recurring problems: a prefix leaked onto the LAN hijacks traffic that should cross the fabric, and during migration a legacy router's advertisement keeps winning over the overlay path you are trying to cut over to.

Detail
  • The intended behaviour. A branch that genuinely owns 10.20.5.0/24 locally should use its local interface, not a fabric path to somewhere else advertising the same prefix.
  • Hijack by accident. A firewall, a test router or an over-broad summary advertising a data centre prefix into the branch LAN wins against the overlay, and branch traffic for that prefix goes to whatever is advertising it — usually nowhere. Routing looks healthy at every layer, which makes it a nasty ticket.
  • Migration friction. At a hybrid site with a legacy CE and a new edge both connected to the LAN, the legacy router's prefixes are learned locally by the LAN protocol and therefore preferred over the fabric. Traffic keeps using the old WAN after you believe you have cut over. This is the single most common migration surprise.
  • What to do about it. Filter deliberately rather than fight distances: tag and filter on redistribution, do not advertise overlapping prefixes into the LAN at a site that also learns them from the fabric, and where you really must change the comparison, adjust the distance of the local protocol for those prefixes rather than trying to make OMP better than everything.

What routing do you need in VPN 0, and what should you keep out of it?Deep

Almost nothing — usually one default route per transport, pointing at the provider. The transport VRF needs to reach exactly two categories of thing: the controllers, and other sites' transport addresses. What must stay out of it is any service-side prefix, because the moment the underlay knows your LAN addressing you have built a second, unencrypted path around the overlay.

Detail
  • What it needs. A default route per transport interface, or a specific route to the controllers and to the remote transport ranges if the provider will not give you a default. On a private MPLS transport the provider may require BGP or OSPF in VPN 0 — that is fine, and it is the one legitimate reason to run a dynamic protocol there.
  • What must not be in it. Service VPN prefixes. If the underlay learns them, traffic can take an unencrypted, unsegmented, unpolicied path straight across the provider network, bypassing the fabric entirely — and it will, silently, because that path may well look better.
  • The one deliberate exception is the default route leaked into a service VPN for direct internet access, which goes the other way — global table into the service VRF, not service into global.
  • Why multiple transports stay separate. Each transport has its own default route and its own colour. Resist the temptation to make the two transports reachable through each other inside VPN 0, because then a failure on one can be masked by a path through the other and your colour-based design stops meaning anything.

How do you run a hybrid site during migration — a legacy CE and a new edge side by side?Deep

Both routers connect to the same LAN and both advertise the site's prefixes to their respective WANs, so the work is entirely in controlling preference in both directions and making sure the forward and return paths agree. The traps are OMP's administrative distance keeping traffic on the legacy path, asymmetric routing breaking anything stateful, and a redistribution loop between the two WANs through the LAN.

The design
  1. Decide the cutover unit. Per site, per VPN, or per application — and prefer per site, because a site either works or it does not, and mixed-path sites are hard to reason about during an incident.
  2. Control the LAN-side preference. The LAN switch learns the data centre prefixes from both routers. Make it prefer the one you want — BGP local preference, OSPF metric, or administrative distance — and change that one knob to cut over. A cutover you can perform and reverse with one routing change is a cutover you can do on a Tuesday.
  3. Control the WAN-side preference. At the data centre, the branch prefixes arrive both over the legacy WAN and over the fabric. Make the data centre prefer the same direction the branch is sending, or you get asymmetry.
  4. Prevent the loop. Both routers redistribute between their WAN and the LAN. Tag on the way out, filter on the way in, both directions, both routers. Without this a prefix can cross from the legacy WAN into the LAN, into the fabric, and back.
  5. Keep the transports apart. If the legacy WAN is the same MPLS service that is also an SD-WAN transport, be careful that the legacy CE's advertisements do not put service prefixes into the transport VRF — the silent-bypass problem from the previous question.
  6. Watch the stateful devices. Firewalls and anything doing NAT or inspection in the middle need the forward and return path to match. Asymmetry during migration is the commonest cause of "some applications broke and some did not".

You cut a site over to SD-WAN and traffic is still using the legacy MPLS. Why?Expert

Almost certainly because the LAN still prefers the legacy router — the prefix in the LAN's routing table points at the old CE, and nothing about the overlay being healthy changes that. The second candidate is OMP's high administrative distance on the edge itself: if the edge also learns the destination locally from the LAN protocol, it prefers that over its own fabric path. Both are ordinary routing preference, not SD-WAN faults.

Where I would look, in order
  1. On the LAN switch, not on the router: show ip route for the destination. Which next hop does it have? If it is the legacy CE, the fabric was never in the running and the cutover change did not take effect — or was applied to the wrong neighbour, or is being beaten by something else.
  2. On the SD-WAN edge: is the route in the service VRF an OMP route, or did the edge learn the destination from the LAN? If the legacy CE is advertising the data centre prefixes into the LAN and the edge is learning them from there, the edge prefers the local path because of OMP's distance — and sends traffic straight back to the LAN, which sends it to the legacy CE.
  3. Is the fabric path actually usable? show sdwan omp routes for the prefix and show sdwan bfd sessions. A path that is unresolved is not a path, and if the only usable route is the legacy one then traffic is doing the correct thing with the information available. This is the case where the cutover change did work and the overlay is the problem.
  4. The data centre end. Even with the branch sending over the fabric, if the data centre prefers the legacy WAN for the return traffic you have asymmetry — which may be what someone is actually reporting, since asymmetry presents as "some things work" rather than as a clean failure.
  5. Policy. A control policy that sets the legacy site's TLOCs, or a data policy pinning traffic, can override routing entirely. show sdwan policy from-vsmart rules it in or out in one command.

12HA & failure handling

The architecture's best property is that the data plane survives the control plane, and the most valuable thing you can know is for how long and what you lose in the meantime. Site-level redundancy is the other half: dual transport and dual router protect against different failures and are not substitutes.

How do you make the controllers redundant?Core

Each component differently, because each has a different amount of state. Validators are nearly stateless — deploy two or more behind a DNS name and you are done. Controllers hold OMP state but rebuild it from the edges, so two or more with edges connecting to several of them covers it. The Manager holds the database and is the hard one: a quorum cluster, and a disaster-recovery pair if the estate justifies it.

Detail

ManagerQuorum cluster — an odd number of nodes; plus a disaster-recovery pair for a second siteNo configuration, no onboarding, no statistics, no visibility — and forwarding unaffected

Spread them properly. Two controllers in the same cloud region, or the same data centre, or on the same hypervisor cluster, are one failure domain regardless of the logical design. Different regions or different providers is the point of having more than one.

What exactly happens if you lose all controllers? For how long is the network fine?Deep

Forwarding continues, because OMP graceful restart keeps the edges using their existing overlay routes — and the default timer is long, many hours. What stops immediately is anything that requires the control plane: new tunnels, route changes, policy changes, onboarding, visibility and key distribution. The network is fine as long as nothing needs to change and the graceful-restart timer has not expired.

"The data plane survives" is true and routinely overstatedWhat survives is the current state. Follow-ups to expect

  • "What is your recovery priority?" — the Manager first, because without it you cannot change anything, including fixing the controllers. Then a Controller, so route and policy changes work again. What they're testingGraceful restart as the mechanism, and the honest limits of "the data plane survives". Ordering the recovery is what distinguishes someone who has planned for this.
What does a Manager cluster give you, and what is disaster recovery for?Deep

A cluster gives redundancy and scale within one site: an odd number of nodes sharing the configuration database, statistics store, messaging and coordination, surviving the loss of a node while keeping quorum. Disaster recovery is for losing the site — a second cluster elsewhere with the configuration replicated, promoted when the primary is gone.

Detail
  • Quorum. An odd number of nodes so a split cannot produce two halves that both think they are authoritative. Lose quorum and the cluster stops accepting changes — which is correct behaviour and feels like an outage.
  • The cluster interface. Inter-node traffic rides a dedicated interface, and latency between nodes matters because the databases are tightly coupled. A cluster stretched across high-latency links is a cluster that will misbehave.
  • Scale. Device count and statistics volume are what drive you from a single node to a cluster. Statistics are usually the thing that grows unexpectedly.
  • Disaster recovery. A second cluster at another site with the configuration database replicated, plus a Validator and Controller there. Failover is a deliberate operation, not automatic — which is appropriate, because promoting the wrong side would be worse than the outage.
  • What DR does not do. It does not make the data plane more resilient; the data plane was already fine. It makes your ability to manage resilient.
The database backup is the thing people neglect, and it is the real recovery planA cluster protects against node failure. It does not protect against a bad configuration push across the estate, a corrupted database, or an upgrade that goes wrong — and for all three, the restore path is a configuration database backup. Which means: take them, store them off the cluster, and test a restore, because an untested backup is a belief rather than a plan. The second neglected item is disk: the statistics database grows, and a Manager whose disk fills stops collecting data and can stop accepting configuration, which arrives as a mysterious inability to push templates. Monitoring Manager disk utilisation is unglamorous and it prevents a genuinely bad day.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "How do you practise a DR failover?" — in a maintenance window, with a plan to fail back, and verifying afterwards that every device reconnected and that templates can still be pushed. A DR capability nobody has exercised is the same category of risk as an untested backup.
  • "Do the Controllers need DR too?" — they need to exist at the DR site, but they hold far less unique state because they rebuild OMP from the edges and take their configuration from the Manager. The Manager's database is the irreplaceable part, which is why DR is framed around it.
What they're testingQuorum, and that DR protects manageability rather than forwarding. The tested-backup and disk-monitoring points are what an operator says.
What does dual transport protect against, and what does dual router protect against?Deep

Different things, and they are not substitutes. Dual transport protects against a circuit or provider failure — one router, two paths. Dual router protects against a device failure, a software upgrade, or a hardware fault — two routers. A site with two circuits into one router still goes down when the router does; a site with two routers each on one circuit loses a transport when either router dies. Full redundancy needs both, plus TLOC extension or dual physical connections so each router can reach each circuit.

The question to ask back"What failure is the business actually worried about, and what is the site worth?" Presenting a tiering model rather than one answer is what a design interview is looking for, and naming the thing that no site-level redundancy protects against — a single building, a single power feed, a single pair of hands — shows you know where the limits are.

You have two data centre hubs. How do branches choose, and how do you handle failover?Deep

Follow-ups to expect

A cloud region hosting your Manager and both Controllers goes down. Walk me through the next four hours.Expert

The first four hours

  2. Minutes 10–20: establish the deadline. Check the graceful-restart timer in use and when sessions dropped. That gives a hard time by which the overlay degrades, and it is the number every subsequent decision is made against.

13NAT traversal

Internet transports are almost always behind NAT, and the overlay's ability to build a direct tunnel depends on what kind. The one fact to carry away: symmetric NAT on both ends makes a direct spoke-to-spoke tunnel impossible, and no amount of configuration changes that.

How does the overlay work when an edge is behind NAT?Core

The edge discovers its own post-NAT address and port by talking to the Validator, which sees the translated source and reports it back. That mapped socket is then advertised in the edge's TLOC, so other edges know where to send tunnel traffic. Both ends initiate toward each other, which punches the mapping open from both sides — the standard NAT-traversal pattern, with the Validator in the role of the reflector that tells each side what it looks like from outside.

Detail
  1. The edge sends control traffic to the Validator from its tunnel interface. The Validator compares the observed source address and port with what the edge claims, and the difference reveals the translation.
  2. The Validator reports back the mapped address, the mapped port and the NAT type it inferred.
  3. The edge advertises both the private and the public socket in its TLOC. Which one a peer uses depends on the colours involved — public colour means post-NAT addressing.
  4. Both edges initiate tunnel traffic toward each other's mapped socket. Because each is sending outbound, each NAT allows the other's return traffic.
  5. A periodic refresh keeps the mapping alive so an idle tunnel does not die when the NAT times the entry out.

Which NAT types work and which break the overlay?Deep

The cone types all work, because the mapping is the same regardless of destination, so the socket the Validator observed is the socket a peer can use. Symmetric NAT breaks it, because the mapping is per destination: the port the Validator saw is not the port that will be used toward another edge, so the peer has nothing usable to send to. Symmetric on both ends is a hard failure; symmetric on one end can sometimes work if the other end is predictably reachable.

Why both ends initiating saves the cone types: a restricted NAT will not accept traffic from a host the inside has not contacted. Since both edges try to build the tunnel, each sends outbound first, each NAT then permits the other's traffic, and the tunnel forms. That is why the restricted types are not a problem despite sounding like one.

Why symmetric is different: the mapped port the Validator reported was allocated for traffic to the Validator. Traffic to a different destination gets a different port. The peer is told a port that will never be the right one, so its packets arrive at a mapping that does not exist.

Carrier-grade NAT is the real-world version of this problemConsumer and small-business broadband increasingly sits behind carrier-grade NAT, which commonly behaves symmetrically and is shared among many subscribers, so you have no control over it and no ability to configure a static translation. The result is a site whose control connections work perfectly — outbound to fixed controllers — and which cannot build direct tunnels to other similarly placed sites. The designs that work are: a hub-and-spoke topology for those sites so all their traffic goes via a hub with a real public address, a second transport that is not carrier-NATed, or a business-grade circuit with a static address where the site justifies it. What does not work is more configuration, and recognising that early saves a lot of wasted effort.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "How do you know which type you have?" — the NAT type line in the local properties, inferred during discovery. If it reports symmetric, believe it and design around it rather than trying to tune past it.
  • "Does this affect hub connectivity?" — no, provided the hub has a real public address. A symmetric-NAT spoke can reach a publicly addressed hub because the spoke initiates and the hub's socket is predictable. It is spoke-to-spoke between two unpredictable mappings that fails, which is exactly why the hub design is the answer.
What they're testingWhy the cone types work (both ends initiate) and why symmetric does not (per-destination mapping). Naming carrier-grade NAT as the practical case is the current, field-relevant answer.
What is the Validator's STUN role, and when do you need port-offset?Deep

Fifty home-worker or micro sites are on consumer broadband behind carrier NAT. Design for them.Expert

Then make the hub capacity and the latency implications part of the design, and give the sites that genuinely need direct connectivity a different circuit.

The design
  1. Hub-and-spoke by policy, not by accident. A control policy that withholds other spokes' TLOCs from these sites, so the fabric does not keep attempting tunnels that cannot form. That stops the futile attempts, removes the confusing BFD-down entries, and makes the topology explicit and documented.
  2. Public colours, always. These are NATed internet transports, so the colour must be public or the sites will advertise unroutable private addresses. This is the single most common configuration error at sites like these.
  3. Publicly addressed hubs with real static addresses, and at least two of them in different failure domains, because every one of these fifty sites now has a hard dependency on hub availability.
  4. Size the hub for the aggregate. Fifty sites' worth of traffic, including any site-to-site traffic that now takes two hops through the hub. If these are home workers running voice and collaboration, that is mostly cloud-bound traffic, which leads to the next point.
  5. Break out cloud traffic locally — direct access for verified SaaS, and a secure gateway for general internet — so the hub carries only genuinely corporate traffic. For home-worker sites this is most of the design's value: hauling collaboration traffic to a hub and back is both slow and expensive.
  6. Latency expectations. Site-to-site via a hub doubles the path. For home workers talking to data centre services that is irrelevant; for two micro sites running a latency-sensitive application between them it is not, and those sites need a different answer — a business circuit with a static address, or acceptance.
  7. Where a site truly needs direct tunnels, the only reliable fix is a circuit you control: a business-grade service with a static public address, or a second transport that is not carrier-NATed. Configuration cannot solve a NAT you do not own.
  8. Tighten the device. Each of these is an internet-connected router in someone's house or a small shop: allow-service minimal, no management exposure, on-box firewall, and no reliance on physical security.

14Edge security

Once a branch breaks out to the internet it is an internet edge, and cEdge can run the controls on-box. The questions that matter: what the container costs in platform resources, how zones map to VPNs, what fail-open means, and why TLS inspection is a bigger decision than it looks.

What security features can run on a cEdge, and what do they cost?Core

A zone-based firewall with application awareness, intrusion prevention, URL filtering, malware protection, TLS decryption, and DNS-layer security through integration with a cloud resolver. The firewall is native to IOS-XE; most of the rest runs in a container on the router, which needs real memory, CPU and often dedicated storage — so the platform has to be sized for it, and an undersized router is the commonest reason a security design does not get deployed.

The self zone, and the pair you forgetTwo traps. First, the self zone governs traffic to the router itself — its management, its routing protocols, its control connections. If you create a zone pair involving the self zone and get it wrong, you can lock yourself out of the device or break its control plane, at a site with nobody to recover it. Treat self-zone policy with the caution you would give an access list on a management interface, and understand the default behaviour before you override it. Second, zone pairs are unidirectional: a pair from CORP to INTERNET inspects outbound and permits the return traffic as part of the session, but it does nothing for traffic initiated from the internet, and it does not create a path from INTERNET to CORP. People add the outbound pair, test a web page, and conclude the firewall is configured — then discover later that a segment they expected to be able to reach a server cannot, because that pair was never created. Enumerate your zone pairs deliberately rather than adding them as symptoms appear.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What about traffic within a VPN?" — not inspected, because it is within a zone. If you need inspection between two subnets in the same segment, they need to be in different zones, which means different VPNs — and that is a segmentation design decision rather than a firewall one. Recognising that the firewall's granularity is bounded by your segmentation is a good observation.
  • "Does this replace a data centre firewall?" — for branch internet traffic, largely yes, and that is the point of a direct-breakout design. For the data centre's own perimeter and for anything needing deep policy, logging at scale and a security team's tooling, no. The branch firewall is about not hauling internet traffic across the WAN, not about replacing the enterprise security estate.
What they're testingThat zones are VPN-based and inter-zone traffic without a pair is dropped. The self-zone caution and the unidirectional nature of pairs are the practical details.
How is intrusion prevention deployed on a cEdge, and what happens if the engine fails?Deep

It runs as a container on the router, hosted alongside IOS-XE with dedicated resources, and traffic you select is diverted into it for inspection. The signature set comes from the Manager, and you choose a policy balancing detection against false positives. The question that matters operationally is fail-open or fail-closed: if the engine stops, does traffic keep flowing uninspected, or does it stop?

Detail
  • Container hosting. The engine needs allocated CPU, memory and storage — on some platforms an added storage device. If the platform cannot provide them, the feature cannot run, which makes this a hardware-selection question at design time.
  • Signature policy. A choice between broader connectivity-preserving sets and stricter security-oriented sets, with the usual trade-off: more signatures means more detections and more false positives, and a false positive at a branch with no local engineer is an outage.
  • Detection or prevention. Detection-only logs; prevention drops. Start in detection, review what it would have blocked, then move to prevention — the same discipline as deploying any inline security control.
  • Updates. Signature updates are distributed through the Manager, which makes them manageable at scale and also means an update is a change to every branch at once. Stage them.
  • Fail behaviour. Fail-open keeps traffic flowing uninspected if the engine dies; fail-closed drops it. Choose deliberately and tell the security team which you chose, because the difference is "degraded security" versus "site outage".

Both fail modes are bad in different ways, so the decision must be consciousFail-open means a crashed or overloaded engine leaves two hundred branches with unfiltered internet access and nobody necessarily noticing, because traffic keeps working — the failure is silent, which is the worst property a security control can have.   • "What is the throughput impact?" — material, and platform-specific. A cloud gateway centralises the policy and the operations at the cost of a hop and a subscription. For most estates the gateway wins on operations; on-box wins where latency or bandwidth makes the hop unattractive, or where there is no internet path to a gateway.
What they're testingContainer hosting with real resource cost, and the fail-open versus fail-closed decision. Staging signature updates is the detail that shows scale experience.
What does TLS decryption on the edge involve, and when would you not do it?Deep

The router terminates the client's TLS session, inspects the plaintext, and opens its own session to the server — which means it must present a certificate the client trusts, so every client needs to trust a CA you control. That is a certificate distribution project, a performance cost on every session, and a privacy and legal question. I would not do it for banking, healthcare, or anything with certificate pinning, and I would not do it at all unless a specific requirement demands it.

Detail
  • Trust. The proxy's issuing CA must be in every client's trust store — managed devices via policy, and unmanaged or guest devices essentially not at all. This is the practical blocker in most estates.
  • Selective decryption. You maintain lists of what to decrypt and what to pass through. The pass-through list exists for categories where decryption is inappropriate or impossible.
  • Pinning and non-browser clients. Applications that pin certificates, mobile apps, and many machine-to-machine clients will simply fail when intercepted — not degrade, fail. Those failures are hard to diagnose because the application reports a generic error.
  • Performance. Terminating and re-establishing TLS for every session is the most expensive thing on this list, and it compounds with whatever inspection you then run on the plaintext.
  • Privacy and law. Decrypting employee traffic has legal and policy implications that vary by jurisdiction, and it needs sign-off from outside the network team. This is not a technical objection but it is the one that most often stops the project.
The answer that shows judgement"I would start from what the inspection is actually for. If it is malware in web downloads, DNS-layer filtering plus reputation and category controls on the metadata gets a lot of it without decryption. If the requirement is genuinely to see inside the payload for a specific set of destinations, decrypt those selectively — and never for financial, health or anything pinned." Then the honest statement: decryption is the control with the highest cost and the most ways to break a user's day, so it should be scoped as narrowly as the requirement allows. Mentioning the legal sign-off unprompted is a strong signal, because it is the part network engineers most often forget exists.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What can you still do without decrypting?" — a surprising amount: the destination, the server name indicated during the handshake, certificate details, reputation of the destination, DNS behaviour, traffic volumes and timing patterns. Many controls and most detections work on that metadata, which is why "we cannot see inside so we are blind" is an overstatement.
  • "Branch or gateway for decryption?" — if you are doing it at all, a cloud gateway is usually the better place: one certificate trust story, one policy, one set of exception lists, and no per-branch performance cost. Doing it on two hundred routers multiplies every part of the problem.
What they're testingThe certificate trust requirement and that pinning breaks outright. Knowing what you can still do without decrypting, and raising legal sign-off, are the mature answers.
You are enabling direct internet access at two hundred branches. Design the security.Expert

Treat it as commissioning two hundred internet edges, which is an operations problem before it is a technology one. The layered answer: protect the router itself, firewall the segments, filter at the DNS layer everywhere because it is nearly free, send general web traffic to a cloud gateway so there is one policy, keep on-box inspection for what the gateway cannot cover, and make sure every branch's logs land where the security team already looks.

The layers
  1. Protect the device. allow-service minimised on the internet TLOC — no SSH, no NETCONF, no routing protocols exposed. Management only over the overlay or the management VRF. This is the layer people skip and it is the one that gets a router compromised.
  2. Firewall the segments. Zone-based firewall with zones from VPNs: corporate outbound inspected, guest outbound restricted to web and DNS, no zone pair at all between guest and corporate, nothing inbound from the internet. Default drop with logging.
  3. DNS-layer filtering at every site. Cheapest and broadest control, works for devices you cannot install software on, and gives per-site visibility of intent even when payloads are encrypted. Force all clients to the intended resolver by permitting DNS only from it and redirecting the rest — otherwise a client using its own public resolver has opted out.
  4. General web traffic to a secure gateway, with two tunnels per branch to different points of presence and a layer 7 tracker, so the security policy is one policy rather than two hundred. Define and document the degraded mode when the gateway is unreachable.
  5. Direct breakout only for an owned, reviewed list of verified SaaS, because that path bypasses the gateway. The list is a security control with a named owner and a review cadence, not a configuration.
  6. On-box inspection where it earns its place — intrusion prevention and URL filtering on platforms sized for it, in detection mode first, with staged signature updates and explicit engine health monitoring. Not everywhere by default.
  7. Logging and monitoring. Branch firewall and filtering logs into the same platform as the rest of the estate. Two hundred sites generating security events nobody aggregates is worse than the backhaul design it replaced, because the security team has lost visibility they used to have.
  8. Standardise and verify by automation. One template, and automated checks that every branch's posture matches it — allow-service settings, zone pairs, resolver enforcement, tracker state. Manual verification does not scale to two hundred and drift is certain.
The framing that wins this question"The technology is the easy part — what changes is that the security team goes from operating one internet edge to being accountable for two hundred, so my design optimises for one policy, one place to look, and automated verification that every site still matches the template." Then name what you would hand the security team on day one: where the logs are, what the degraded mode looks like when the gateway is unreachable, who owns the direct-breakout list, and how posture drift is detected. Candidates who answer with a feature list have described a configuration; candidates who answer with the operating model have described a design, and this question exists to tell them apart.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What is the first thing you would turn on?" — DNS-layer filtering and tightened allow-service, before any breakout traffic flows. Both are cheap, neither needs a container, and together they remove the largest and most embarrassing risks. Enabling breakout before them is the sequencing mistake.
  • "How do you handle the branch with no gateway reachability?" — it falls back to on-box controls plus DNS filtering, which you have already deployed everywhere precisely so that the degraded mode is not "no security". Designing the fallback as a deliberate posture rather than an absence is the point.
What they're testingWhether you see this as an operations problem: one policy, aggregated logging, automated posture verification. Protecting the router itself and sequencing DNS filtering first are the two specifics that mark real deployment experience.

15Multicast & AppQoE

Three services that come up less often and discriminate strongly when they do: multicast over an overlay built from unicast tunnels, application optimisation with its real resource cost, and flow export — which is how you answer "what is actually on my WAN" rather than guessing.

How does multicast work over an SD-WAN overlay?Deep

The overlay is built from unicast tunnels, so multicast cannot simply be flooded — the fabric uses a replicator. One or more edges are designated as replicators; the source site sends a single copy to a replicator, and the replicator sends a unicast copy to each site that has interested receivers. PIM runs on the service side as normal, and the fabric discovers which sites want which groups through OMP.

Detail
  1. Service side is ordinary PIM. Receivers join with IGMP, the local edge runs PIM sparse mode toward the LAN, and a rendezvous point exists somewhere — usually on the service side at a data centre.
  2. The fabric discovers interest. Edges advertise multicast capability and group membership through OMP, so the control plane knows which sites need which groups without any multicast in the underlay.
  3. Replication. The source's edge sends one copy to a replicator. The replicator fans it out over unicast tunnels to each interested site. No multicast is required in the provider network at all, which is the main attraction — you are not dependent on the carrier supporting multicast.
  4. Replicator placement and capacity. A replicator's load is the stream rate times the number of receiving sites, so a 4 Mbps stream to a hundred sites is 400 Mbps out of one device. Placement and sizing are the entire design problem.
  5. Multiple replicators with a threshold for how many sites each will serve, so load spreads and no single device is the whole multicast fabric.

Replication turns a multicast design into a bandwidth designThe whole point of multicast is sending one copy; replication over unicast tunnels sends one copy per site, so the saving exists on the LAN and at the source but not across the WAN out of the replicator. A design that assumed multicast would be cheap — a corporate broadcast to every branch, say — discovers that the replicator's uplink is the constraint and that the aggregate WAN cost is the same as unicast. The consequences: size the replicator's circuit for stream rate times site count, use several replicators with thresholds, and for a genuinely large one-to-many distribution consider whether a content delivery approach or a cloud streaming service is simply the better architecture. Saying that unprompted is a strong signal, because the enthusiasm for "multicast over SD-WAN" usually skips the arithmetic.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "Where would you put the rendezvous point?" — on the service side at a data centre, reachable over the fabric, with a static address for predictability and a redundant one if the application matters. Dynamic RP discovery works but adds a dependency you have to debug across an overlay.
  • "What if the provider does support multicast?" — it does not help, because the overlay's traffic is encrypted unicast between tunnel endpoints; the provider cannot replicate what it cannot see. That independence from the carrier is the trade you are making, and it is worth stating explicitly.
What they're testingThe replicator model and that no underlay multicast is needed. The bandwidth arithmetic at the replicator is the insight that distinguishes a real answer.
What is AppQoE? Explain TCP optimisation and DRE.Deep

AppQoE is the application-optimisation set on cEdge. TCP optimisation has the edge proxy the TCP session so it can apply better window and loss-recovery behaviour than the endpoints would — which helps most on long, lossy paths. Data redundancy elimination deduplicates repeated byte patterns against a local cache, so repeated content crosses the WAN once. Both can run on the router itself or be offloaded to a separate service node, and both cost real resources.

Encryption has eroded most of DRE's value, and saying so is the honest answerDeduplication works by recognising repeated byte patterns. It also complicates troubleshooting, because the flow you see on one side is not literally the flow on the other. Worth knowing before enabling it broadly.

The business wants a live all-hands video stream to two hundred branches. What do you tell them?Expert

That multicast over the overlay will not save the bandwidth they are hoping for, because replication happens over unicast tunnels — so a 4 Mbps stream to two hundred sites is 800 Mbps leaving the replicator and 4 Mbps arriving at each branch, every time. Then I would offer the architectures that do work: a cloud streaming platform the branches reach over their internet breakout, or a content delivery approach with local caching, with WAN multicast as a distant third.

What I would say and ask
  1. State the arithmetic first. Stream rate times site count. That single number usually ends the multicast conversation, and it should be the first thing on the table rather than something discovered during the event.
  2. Ask what the stream rate actually needs to be. There is a large difference between a talking-head stream and a high-definition production, and the business often has no view until asked. Reducing the rate is the cheapest intervention available.
  3. Ask whether branches have internet breakout. If they do — and in a modern SD-WAN design they should — a cloud platform removes the problem entirely, and the WAN question disappears.
  4. Check each branch's downlink. A 4 Mbps stream into a 10 Mbps MPLS circuit that also carries voice and business traffic is not viable regardless of how it is distributed. Per-tunnel QoS at the hub and a low-priority class for the stream are mandatory if it does cross the WAN.
  5. Plan for the simultaneity. Two hundred sites all starting at the same moment is the worst case for every shared resource in the path — replicator uplink, hub capacity, gateway capacity. A rehearsal at scale, not a test with three sites, is what tells you whether it works.
  6. If it must cross the WAN: multiple replicators with thresholds, the stream in the lowest-priority class so it cannot damage voice or business traffic, and a stated expectation that quality degrades before anything else does.
Why this is a good interview questionIt tests whether you will answer the question asked — "can you do multicast" — or the question meant, which is "how do two hundred sites watch this". The strong answer leads with the arithmetic, offers the architecture that avoids the problem, and only then explains how to do it on the WAN if that is genuinely required. It also tests whether you push back constructively: telling the business "yes, and it will consume 800 Mbps of hub capacity and degrade voice at the small branches" is more useful than either a flat no or an unqualified yes, and it is the kind of answer that gets an engineer invited to the planning meeting next time.
What they're testingWhether you know replication is unicast and can do the arithmetic out loud, and whether you redirect to the architecture that actually solves it. Planning for the simultaneous start is the operational detail.

The Manager shows a device offline but users say everything works. Explain and act.Deep

That is exactly what a control-plane failure looks like from a management console: the device has lost its session to the Manager and possibly the Controllers, while OMP graceful restart keeps the overlay routes alive and the data plane forwarding. The users are right and the dashboard is right — they are reporting different planes. The important consequence is that a clock is now running, and I need to restore the control plane before graceful restart expires.

What to do
  1. Confirm the data plane from the device or a peer. BFD sessions up, OMP routes present, tunnel counters incrementing. If so, say clearly that traffic is unaffected.
  2. Establish why the sessions dropped. If the device is reachable out of band: show sdwan control connections-history for the error codes. If not reachable at all, the transport carrying control traffic is the suspect.
  3. Check the usual causes. A certificate that expired, a clock that drifted, a firewall change on the path, the Manager's own health, or a transport where max-control-connections was set to zero leaving no control path when the other transport failed.

  5. Restore. Fix the path or the trust problem. Once a Controller is reachable, OMP re-converges from the edge's own state and the deadline stops mattering.
  6. Afterwards, check what the Manager thinks the device's configuration is — a device that was offline through a template push may be out of step and will receive it on reconnection, which is a change you want to be awake for.

An application is intermittently slow from one branch. Everything looks green. How do you find it?Deep

Intermittent and green means the measurements you are looking at are averaged over too long a window, or they are measuring something other than what the user experiences. So I would narrow in time first — when exactly, and does it correlate with anything — then look at per-tunnel statistics over a short window, then QoS drops, then whether the flow is even taking the path I expect, using the policy simulation and packet-trace tools rather than inference.

The approach
  1. Pin down the when. Business hours only, a particular time of day, or random? Time-of-day correlation points at congestion or a scheduled job; overnight points at NAT timeouts or backups; random points at the path or the application.
  2. Short-window tunnel statistics. The SLA view is averaged over a long period by default. Look at the raw loss, latency and jitter per tunnel, and at the BFD history for flaps that the average hides.
  3. QoS drops. If the circuit is saturated at those times, the application's class may be queued or dropped while the circuit looks "fine" in aggregate. Policy-map counters and platform drop statistics.
  4. What path is the flow actually taking? Do not infer it — simulate it. The policy simulation commands tell you the chosen tunnel and service path for a specific five-tuple, which settles whether policy or application-aware routing is moving it somewhere unexpected.
  5. Trace real packets. Conditional packet tracing on the edge shows what actually happens to a flow inside the forwarding path — which feature dropped it, what it was rewritten to, which interface it left by. This is the tool that turns "the network looks fine" into a fact.
  6. Check the application's own view. Is it slow from other sites too? If so it is not this branch. Is it slow over the LAN at the branch? Then it is not the WAN. Eliminating the network properly is as valuable as finding a network fault.

An edge has high CPU and its control connections keep flapping. Work it.Deep

Find out which process is consuming it, because the causes divide cleanly: the forwarding path being overloaded by traffic or by a feature like inspection or optimisation, the control plane being overloaded by OMP churn or an excessive route count, or a punt path problem where traffic that should be hardware-switched is reaching the CPU. Each has a different fix and a different command to identify it.

The investigation
  1. Which process? Sorted process CPU, plus platform resource utilisation. A control-plane process means OMP, policy or session churn. A forwarding process means traffic or features.
  2. Is it the forwarding engine? Platform statistics for the data path, including drops and punt reasons. Traffic being punted to the CPU — because of an unsupported feature combination, fragmentation, or an exception path — is a classic cause of high CPU with modest traffic.
  3. Is it OMP churn? Route and TLOC counts, and whether they are stable. A flapping remote site, an unstable redistribution, or a route count far beyond what the platform should hold will keep the control plane busy. show sdwan omp summary and the route counts over time.
  4. Is it the security or optimisation container? Check its resource usage. An undersized platform running inspection is the single most common cause of this symptom on a cEdge, and the fix is a feature or a platform decision rather than a tuning exercise.
  5. Is the flapping a cause or an effect? High CPU can starve BFD and control sessions, producing flaps — so the flaps may be a symptom. Conversely, flapping sessions generate churn that raises CPU. Establish the order from the logs and timestamps.
  6. Route count sanity. How many prefixes is this branch holding, and does it need them? VPN membership policy and summarisation at the source are the structural fixes if the answer is "far too many".

The questions a senior candidate is really being hired for: how to size it, how to get there from an MPLS estate without a bad weekend, when SD-WAN is the wrong answer, and how the thing is operated once it is built.

How do you size an SD-WAN deployment? What are the scale limits?Deep

Count the things that multiply. Device count drives Manager sizing and clustering. Control sessions — transports times controllers times sites — drive controller count and whether you need affinity. Tunnel count per edge — local TLOCs times remote TLOCs — drives platform selection. And route and path counts drive both OMP state and the advertisement limits. The specific verified numbers change per release, so the professional answer is to check the release's verified scale document and design with headroom, not to quote figures from memory.

Plan a migration from a traditional MPLS estate with DMVPN backup to SD-WAN.Deep

Data centres first, because branches need something to connect to; then a small pilot including a real production site; then branch waves with hybrid coexistence so each site can be cut over and reversed independently; then decommission. The MPLS circuits become SD-WAN transports rather than being replaced, which is what makes the migration incremental instead of a flag day.

The phases
  1. Design and inventory. Segmentation plan — how many VPNs and why; addressing; colour scheme; site ID allocation with room to grow; the policy model; the security model for breakout. Site IDs and colours are decisions you will live with, so get them right before the first device.
  2. Controllers. Cloud-hosted or on-premises, across failure domains, with certificates and the authorised list in place. Deliberately boring, and the foundation for everything after it.
  3. Data centres and hubs first. Until a hub is in the fabric there is nowhere for a branch to connect. Run the hub alongside the legacy WAN, with both paths to the data centre LAN and controlled preference between them.
  4. Pilot. Three to five sites — and include one that matters, because a pilot of three quiet sites proves nothing about the design under load or under failure. Test failover, failback, voice quality, the policy, and the rollback procedure itself.
  5. Branch waves. Group by region or site type. At each site: deploy the edge alongside the existing router, bring up control connections and tunnels, verify, then cut over with one reversible routing change. Keep the legacy path available for a defined period.
  6. Enable the features that justify the project. Local breakout, application-aware routing, SIG. Do this after connectivity is stable, not during the cutover — otherwise every problem has several possible causes.
  7. Decommission. Remove legacy advertisements, return circuits or repurpose them as transports, and tidy up the routing that existed only to support coexistence. A migration is not finished until this is done, and half-decommissioned estates are a persistent source of confusion.
The two principles that make a migration survivableFirst, MPLS becomes a transport, not a casualty. You are not replacing the circuit, you are putting an overlay on it — so there is no moment where a site loses its WAN. Second, every cutover is one reversible change, prepared and tested in advance: a routing preference at the branch and the matching one at the data centre, with a verified traceroute as the test. Sites whose cutover requires a configuration session during the window will produce incidents. And the ordering rule worth stating: get connectivity stable everywhere before enabling the clever features, because debugging application-aware routing at a site whose tunnels are not yet reliable is a waste of everyone's evening.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "How long do you keep the legacy path?" — long enough to have seen the site through a normal business cycle and at least one failure, and short enough that nobody treats it as permanent. A defined date in the plan, because an undated "we will remove it later" never happens and you end up paying for both for years.
  • "What usually goes wrong?" — the LAN still preferring the legacy router, asymmetric routing through stateful devices, MTU on the new overlay, and sites where the circuit hand-off is not what the records said. All four are predictable, and all four are worth a pre-migration check per site.
What they're testingData centres first, a meaningful pilot, reversible per-site cutovers, and features after stability. "MPLS becomes a transport" is the framing that shows you have done this.
When is SD-WAN the wrong answer?Deep

When the estate is too small for the overhead to pay back, when the operating model cannot support it, when the requirement is layer 2 extension rather than routed connectivity, or when the transport reality defeats the premise — a single circuit per site with no internet alternative gets most of the benefit from nothing the fabric does. Being able to say this is worth more in an interview than enthusiasm, because every vendor-led conversation assumes the answer is yes.

How is the fabric actually operated day to day? Templates, config groups, change control.Deep

Everything comes from the Manager: devices are configured by template or config group, local CLI changes are overwritten or refused, and centralized policy exists as a single active version for the whole fabric. That shapes the operating model entirely — standardisation becomes mandatory rather than aspirational, and every policy change is a fabric-wide change that needs proper control.

Detail
  • Templates or config groups. The older model attaches feature templates through a device template per device; the current model uses config groups with feature profiles and per-device variables. Either way the principle is the same: a small number of standard configurations with variables for the per-site values, and a CLI add-on for the genuinely exceptional.
  • The exception problem. Every site that needs a bespoke template is a site outside your standard, and estates accumulate these until there are forty templates and nobody knows which is current. Discipline here is the difference between an estate you can change safely and one you cannot.
  • Centralized policy change control. One active policy means every change rewrites the whole thing. Keep the policy text in version control outside the Manager so changes can be diffed and reviewed, define a pilot site list for new behaviour, and rehearse the rollback.
  • Software lifecycle. Images, staged activation, and a rollback path. Upgrades are distributed from the Manager, which makes them manageable and also makes it possible to break many sites at once — so wave them.
  • Monitoring that matches the architecture. Control connections, BFD state per tunnel, certificate expiry, Manager disk and cluster health, SIG tunnel trackers, and the security engines' health if you run them. Several of these fail silently, which is exactly why they need explicit monitoring.
  • Documentation that is operationally useful. Site ID allocation, colour scheme, the policy's intent, who owns the direct-breakout list, and what the degraded modes look like. The topology diagram matters less than these.
The sentence that shows you have operated one"Because configuration comes from templates and there is one active policy, the operating model has to be standardisation plus proper change control — a fabric where every site is a special case is a fabric you cannot change safely." Then name the three things that fail silently and therefore need explicit monitoring: certificate expiry, a data-only transport with no usable mapping, and a SIG tunnel whose service is broken behind a healthy tunnel. Candidates who talk about the operating model rather than the feature set are the ones who have run this in production.
What they're testingThat template-driven configuration and single-active-policy force a standardised operating model. Naming the silent failures that need monitoring is the operator's answer.

  1. One template per store type, zero exceptions tolerated. Five hundred identical stores is the gift this environment gives you — do not squander it on per-site specials.
  2. Zero-touch provisioning, so a store install is a courier and a shop manager plugging in a box, not an engineer visit. At five hundred sites this is the difference between a feasible rollout and an impossible one.
  3. Automated posture verification — every store checked against the template for firewall policy, allow-service, segment isolation, tracker state and certificate validity. Manual verification does not scale and drift is certain.
  4. Controllers across failure domains, affinity by region, Manager clustered with a tested disaster-recovery path. Five hundred stores is enough that a control-plane incident is a board-level event.
  5. Waved change. Policy pilots, staged software, staged signature updates. Anything that reaches all five hundred at once will eventually reach all five hundred at once badly.
What to lead with and what to askLead with the topology decision and its reason: "stores do not talk to each other, so regional hub-and-spoke, and that also solves the tunnel arithmetic." Then the payment segment, because in retail the compliance scope is the highest-value thing the network design can influence — keeping that segment isolated, hub-only and inspected reduces audit scope at every one of five hundred sites. Then ask: what the payment application actually requires, whether tills must work during a WAN outage (which may mean local processing rather than any network answer), what the stores' real circuit options are by geography, and who will operate this on a Sunday. That last question shapes the design as much as any of the technical ones.
What they're testingWhether you avoid a global mesh with a reason, drive segmentation from the compliance boundary, and treat five hundred identical sites as an automation problem. Asking whether tills must survive a WAN outage is the question that marks a designer.

18Reference topology

One fabric with two transports, two regional hubs, three kinds of branch and a cloud security gateway. Four questions asked against it — narrate it, trace a transaction, predict a failure, improve it — which is exactly the shape of an SD-WAN whiteboard round.

Reference fabric · regional hub-and-spoke, two transports, three branch tiers, one segment that never breaks out.

Walk me through this topology.Deep

Controllers in two regions reached over the internet transport, with branches preferring their regional controller by affinity. Two transports — MPLS as a private colour and the internet as a public one. Two hubs, each hosting a firewall as a service and one acting as a multicast replicator. Three branch tiers, deliberately different. And the thing I would raise immediately is the payments segment, which never breaks out — that single decision is doing most of the compliance work in this design.

Block by block
  1. Controllers. Validators, a Manager cluster and two Controllers, split across two regions so a regional failure does not take the control plane with it. Affinity sends east branches to the east Controller and west to west, with cross-region fallback. Reached over the internet transport, which is worth noting: if a site's only working transport is MPLS and the Controllers are internet-reachable only, that site has no control path.
  2. Transports. MPLS with a private colour, so sites exchange private addresses with each other on it. The internet with a public colour, so they exchange post-NAT addresses. Getting those two the wrong way round is the classic failure, and the annotation in the picture is there because it matters.
  3. Hubs. Both advertise the data centre prefixes with their own TLOCs, so every branch has multiple paths and chooses by preference — here, regional. Both host a firewall advertised as a service, which is how payments traffic gets inspected. One is a multicast replicator.
  4. Large store. Two routers, one transport each physically, TLOC extension so each can use both — so it survives a router failure, a circuit failure and a software upgrade. The inter-router link is a dependency to design properly.
  5. Standard store. One router, two transports. Survives a circuit failure, not a router failure. That is a deliberate, costed decision rather than an oversight.
  6. Micro site. Broadband and cellular, behind carrier NAT, so direct tunnels to other micro sites are impossible — hub-only by explicit policy rather than by silent failure.
  7. Cloud. A verified SaaS list breaking out directly, everything else through a secure gateway with two tunnels and a layer 7 tracker. The payments segment is excluded from both.
  8. Segments. Four: payments, store operations, guest and management — driven by the compliance boundary and the guest boundary, not by the organisation chart.
Close with the observation, not the inventory"The design decision I would defend hardest is that payments is hub-only with no breakout and no leaking — it keeps the compliance scope small at every one of these sites, and it is the one segment where I would refuse every future request for an exception." Narrating a diagram is a skill in itself: go outside-in or top-down, name what each choice costs, and finish with a judgement rather than trailing off. An interviewer who has to prompt you for an opinion has learned less than one you volunteer it to.
What they're testingStructured narration, the colour/addressing pairing, and whether you spot the compliance-driven segmentation. Noting the controller reachability dependency is the senior detail.
Trace a card payment from a till at a standard store to the payment application at DC-EAST.Deep

It stays in the payments VRF the whole way: a route lookup in that VRF finds an OMP route whose next hop is a DC-EAST TLOC, application-aware routing picks the tunnel that currently meets the payments SLA, the packet gets the VPN 10 label and is encrypted to the hub, and at the hub the control policy's service action sends it through the firewall before it reaches the application. No breakout, no leaking, and the firewall is in the path by policy rather than by routing accident.

The trace
  1. Till to the edge. The till's VLAN is bound to VRF 10 on the store's edge. Inbound localized policy applies — marking, and an access list that permits only what payments traffic should be.
  2. Route lookup in VRF 10. The match is an OMP route for the payment application's prefix, next hop a DC-EAST TLOC — preferred over DC-WEST by the regional control policy.
  3. Application-aware routing decides the tunnel. The payments SLA class is evaluated against the measured loss, latency and jitter of the MPLS and internet tunnels to that hub. MPLS is preferred; if it is failing the SLA, the internet tunnel is used. No strict — a degraded path is better than a failed transaction.
  4. Encapsulation. The VPN 10 label, then ESP, then UDP from the chosen TLOC's port, then the outer IP header. The outer DSCP is set by the rewrite rule to whatever the provider honours for this class.
  5. Across the transport as ordinary encrypted UDP between two transport addresses. The provider sees nothing of VRFs or card data.
  6. At DC-EAST. Decrypt, read the label, land in VRF 10. The control policy's service action means the traffic is handed to the firewall in that VRF, inspected, returned, and then routed to the application.
  7. Return traffic reverses it — and the return path's tunnel is chosen by DC-EAST's own measurements, so it may not be the same transport. For a TCP transaction that is fine; it matters if anything in the middle is stateful.

If the firewall service route is withdrawn, where does the payment go?This is the question to anticipate. With service tracking enabled, the hub withdraws the firewall service route when the appliance stops responding — and then the traffic takes the direct path to the application, uninspected. Routing works, the transaction succeeds, and the compliance control has silently disappeared. For a payments segment that is very likely the wrong behaviour: you would want tloc-action strict so the traffic follows the service path or not at all, accepting a payments outage over an uninspected payments flow — and that is a decision for the security and compliance owners, not the network team, documented and signed off. Volunteering this failure mode on a payments trace is one of the strongest things you can do in this question.
What they're testingLabel-based segmentation, application-aware routing choosing the tunnel, and the service insertion at the hub. The fail-open service-route question is the expert addition.
A standard store's broadband circuit fails. Give me the timeline, and say what the users notice.Expert

Overlay traffic moves to MPLS within about seven seconds on default BFD timers, so corporate and payments traffic survives with a brief interruption. What does not survive is anything that depended on that circuit specifically: the secure gateway tunnels, direct SaaS breakout, and guest wi-fi — because guest has no fallback by design. So the users who notice are the guests, and the staff using cloud applications, depending on whether internet traffic falls back over the overlay.

The two things worth saying that nobody expectsFirst, capacity: failover is not free. A store running comfortably across 10 Mbps of MPLS and 50 Mbps of broadband does not run comfortably on 10 Mbps, so the honest answer is "connectivity survives and performance degrades, and whether that is acceptable depends on what we chose to protect in the QoS policy". Second, the control path: if MPLS was configured as a data-only transport to save control overhead, the site keeps forwarding and becomes unmanageable at the exact moment you want to look at it. Both are design consequences rather than faults, and raising them turns a recitation of BFD timers into a design discussion — which is what an expert question is for.
Follow-ups to expect
  • "What if MPLS had failed instead?" — broadband carries everything, which it has the capacity for, and the secure gateway and breakout keep working. Arguably the better failure. Worth saying, because it shows you think about which failure you would rather have, and it is an argument for broadband-primary designs.
  • "How would you know it happened?" — BFD session alarms, the tunnel history, and the gateway tracker. And the tracker matters here: it is what distinguishes "the circuit died" from "the gateway's service died", which look identical from the user's side and have completely different fixes.
What they're testingThe BFD-driven timeline, and that different services fail differently. Capacity after failover and the control-path risk are the two points that mark a designer rather than an operator.
What would you change about this design?Expert

Four things, ordered by risk reduced: make sure every site has a control path that survives any single transport failure, verify the micro sites are hub-only by policy rather than by accident, put a hard fail-closed decision on the payments firewall service, and check that the hubs and the MPLS circuits are sized for the failover case rather than the steady state. The first three are configuration; the fourth is a capacity conversation.

In priority order
  1. Control-path survivability. The Controllers are reached over the internet transport. If a site's internet circuit fails and MPLS does not carry control connections, that site keeps forwarding and becomes unmanageable — and nobody notices because users are fine. I would verify that every tier has at least one control path per transport, or make the Controllers reachable over MPLS too.
  2. Micro sites: hub-only by policy. Behind carrier NAT they cannot build direct tunnels to each other, so the fabric will keep trying and failing. A control policy rejecting their mutual TLOCs stops the futile attempts and, more importantly, stops permanently noisy BFD alarms that train everyone to ignore BFD alarms.
  3. Decide fail-closed on the payments firewall service. Today, if the firewall service route is withdrawn, payments traffic takes the direct uninspected path. For a compliance-scoped segment that is almost certainly wrong. Make it strict, get it signed off, and document that a firewall failure is a payments outage — which is a decision the business should make knowingly.
  4. Size for failover, not steady state. Two hubs active means each carries half; a hub failure doubles the survivor's load. A standard store on 10 Mbps of MPLS plus 50 Mbps of broadband cannot deliver its steady-state experience on MPLS alone. Both are capacity conversations to have before the first failure, not after.
Smaller things I would check first
  • That the micro sites' and stores' internet transports use a public colour, and the MPLS a private one — the single most common configuration error, and it is one command to confirm.
  • That the data-only cellular transport at the micro sites has the Validator as a STUN server and the STUN service permitted, or its TLOC will never be usable and the backup does not exist.
  • The application-aware routing poll interval, because on the default the SLA average covers about an hour and payments would never be moved off a degrading circuit in time to matter.
  • Advertisement and ECMP path limits against the real path count — two hubs times two transports, per prefix, per VPN.
  • Certificate expiry monitoring, Manager disk, and the gateway trackers — the three things that fail silently.
Lead with the thing that is invisibleOf everything on this list, the control-path risk is the one worth naming first, precisely because it produces no symptom: traffic flows, users are happy, and the site is quietly unmanageable with a graceful-restart clock running. Interviewers remember a candidate who finds the failure that nobody would notice. Then be honest about effort: the colour check and the poll interval are this-afternoon fixes, the fail-closed decision is a conversation with the compliance owner, and the capacity work is a budget cycle. Separating them that way is what makes a review actionable rather than a wish list.
What they're testingWhether you find the silent control-path problem, make the micro-site topology explicit, force the fail-closed decision, and raise failover capacity. Separating quick fixes from projects is the senior framing.

19Whiteboard scenarios

Open-ended design and diagnosis. Ask about the constraints before drawing, state your assumptions, and name the trade-offs you are choosing between rather than picking silently.

Draw an SD-WAN design for a company with a data centre, forty branches, and a growing cloud footprint.Expert

Ask four things first — what the cloud footprint actually is, whether branches can get a second circuit, what the security team can operate, and whether any application needs branch-to-branch latency. Then draw: controllers across two failure domains, two transports per branch, the data centre as a hub plus a second egress for resilience, meshed branches because forty is well within scale, local breakout to a secure gateway, and segmentation driven by whatever the real policy boundaries are.

What I would ask before drawing
  1. What is "cloud"? SaaS only, or workloads in a provider? SaaS means breakout and Cloud OnRamp for SaaS. Workloads mean extending the fabric into the provider, which is a much bigger piece of work.
  2. Two circuits per branch, or one? It changes everything about path selection, and the answer is often "one in some places" which means a tiered design.
  3. Who operates the security? Decides on-box inspection versus a secure gateway, and that decision shapes the branch platform choice.
  4. Any branch-to-branch requirement? Voice between branches, or a distributed application. If none, hub transit is acceptable and the design simplifies.

Voice quality has degraded across the estate since last Tuesday. Nothing was changed. Find it.Expert

"Nothing was changed" is almost never true, so I would establish what did change — including things that change themselves: a signature update, a certificate rotation, a provider maintenance, a seasonal traffic increase, a new application deployed by someone else. Then work the measurement question, because estate-wide voice degradation with green dashboards usually means the measurement window is hiding it or the QoS is no longer protecting voice.

The investigation
  1. Scope it precisely. All sites or a subset? All calls or branch-to-branch only? Both directions? One transport? A pattern that correlates with a provider, a region or a colour names the cause.
  2. Find what changed itself. Signature or software updates pushed from the Manager, a certificate rotation, a provider change window, a new application or a new backup schedule consuming capacity. Also: did someone add sites, raising hub load?
  3. Short-window measurements. Per-tunnel loss, latency and jitter over minutes, not the SLA average over an hour. And BFD history, for flaps the average hides.
  4. Is voice still being classified as voice? Counters on the voice sequence. A changed application, a new client version, or an updated recognition signature set can quietly stop matching — and then the SLA class is irrelevant because nothing is in it. This is a genuinely common cause of "nothing changed".
  5. Is QoS still protecting it? Policy-map counters and queue drops. A new high-volume application can saturate a class, and the outer DSCP marking may not be what you assume — take a capture rather than reading the rewrite rule.
  6. Capacity. Compare circuit utilisation with a month ago. Estate-wide degradation that tracks a utilisation increase is a capacity problem wearing a quality problem's clothes.
  7. Correlate with the voice platform. Its own call-quality statistics, matched to the network measurements by time. If its figures are clean, the problem may not be the network at all — and establishing that credibly is as valuable as finding a fault.
The two causes to name as most likelyFirst, classification stopped matching — a client or signature change means voice is no longer recognised, so it falls out of its SLA class and its queue, and everything about the configuration still looks correct. Second, capacity crept, so the QoS policy is now doing real work where it used to have headroom, and the class you under-provisioned is the one that hurts. Both fit "nothing was changed" exactly, both are invisible on a dashboard showing green, and both are found by checking counters rather than configuration. Leading with those two, then the systematic scope-and-correlate method, is a strong answer to a deliberately vague question.
What they're testingWhether you treat "nothing changed" sceptically, scope before diving, and check that policy is still matching. Correlating with the voice platform's own data is the completeness marker.
The business wants to drop MPLS entirely and run on two broadband circuits per site. Advise them.Expert

It is often the right move and it is not free, so I would answer with the conditions under which I would support it. Two genuinely independent broadband circuits — different providers and different physical paths — application-aware routing tuned to react in time, realistic expectations about what happens when both circuits are mediocre at once, and a plan for the sites where broadband is not good enough. Then quantify what is actually being given up, which is usually less than people fear and more than the vendor slide suggests.

My conditions for supporting it
  1. Genuine diversity. Two circuits from two providers that do not share a last mile, a duct or an exchange. Two broadband circuits from the same provider down the same street is one circuit with two bills, and finding that out during an outage is expensive.
  2. Measure first. Run application-aware routing with the MPLS still in place and collect per-tunnel loss, latency and jitter for a quarter. That data settles the argument either way, and it costs nothing but time.
  3. Tune the poll interval. On the default, the SLA average covers about an hour, so the mechanism you are relying on to make broadband acceptable will not react in time. This is non-negotiable for a broadband-only design.
  4. Plan for both circuits being bad together. Local congestion in an area affects both. Decide what happens: degrade, or add cellular as a third path for critical traffic at the sites that justify it.
  5. Exceptions by site. Some sites will not have acceptable broadband. Keep MPLS, or a business-grade service, where the measurement says so — a uniform policy that ignores the data is how a good decision becomes a bad rollout.
  6. Security follows. Dropping MPLS usually means everything is on the internet, so the breakout security design becomes load-bearing rather than optional.
The framing that gets you listened toDo not argue from loyalty to MPLS. "I support this, and here is what has to be true, and here is how we will know" is far more persuasive than a list of risks — and the measure-first condition is the one that makes the whole conversation evidence-based rather than a contest of opinions. Also correct the private-addressing misconception explicitly, because it comes up in every one of these discussions and conceding it needlessly weakens the rest of your case.
What they're testingWhether you can support a cost-driven decision with conditions, insist on circuit diversity, and require measurement before commitment. Tuning the poll interval as a hard requirement is the technical detail that proves you have run it.
